## Core method / idea

The paper is a bias-measurement study, not a benchmark or a system paper — it is read here purely as the branch's **circularity reference**, i.e. as evidence for what actually causes LLM-judge bias so that our Q3 anti-circularity argument is grounded in a real finding rather than an assumption.

1. **Self-preference bias metric (Definition 4.1, §4.1, Eq. 2).** Setup: an LLM evaluator `f` compares two dialogue responses (A/B) to the same prompt and picks a winner. Let `Y ∈ {0,1}` = index of the human-preferred response, `Y' ∈ {0,1}` = index of the response `f` picks, `S ∈ {0,1}` = index of the response that `f` itself generated. Restricting to cases where the human prefers response 1 (`Y=1`):
   `Bias = P(Y'=1 | S=1, Y=1) − P(Y'=1 | S=0, Y=1)`
   i.e., the difference in the evaluator's *hit rate at correctly picking the human-preferred response*, split by whether that human-preferred response happens to be the evaluator's own output (`S=1`) or someone else's (`S=0`). This is explicitly built on the **Equal Opportunity** fairness definition (§3, citing Hardt et al. 2016), not Demographic Parity — the authors are deliberate about this choice (see §6 below). 0 = no bias; →1 = strong self-preference; negative = reverse bias (undervaluing own outputs).
2. **Experimental setting (§4.2).** 8 LLM evaluators: GPT-4, GPT-3.5-Turbo, Vicuna-7B, Vicuna-13B, oasst-pythia-12B, dolly-v2-12B, Koala-13B, stablelm-tuned-alpha-7B. Same 8 models are also used as *generators* (response-pair members), later extended with Llama-2 and Llama-3 as generators only (§5). Data: Chatbot Arena dataset (Zheng et al. 2024, MT-Bench/Chatbot-Arena paper), 33,000 dialogues, each a user query + a pair of responses from two different LLMs, pre-labeled by human raters ("model_a"/"model_b"/"tie"). Evaluators score via `scoreA = p(A|context) / [p(A|context) + p(B|context)]` (Eq. 3) — i.e., token-likelihood-based preference, not necessarily a free-text "[[A]]" verdict for every model (though verdict-format models are also used and non-conforming outputs excluded, §4.2). Position bias controlled by swapping A/B and averaging over two orderings (§4.2) — this is the paper's *only* explicit judge-bias control, notably narrower than what Q17 asks for (no mention of multiple independent judges as a control, no anonymization procedure beyond position swap, no held-out human-labelled divergence check as a *mitigation*, only as the base data source).
3. **How overestimation happens — perplexity hypothesis (§5).** Hypothesis: LLMs favor outputs more familiar to them, operationalized as lower perplexity (of the response, under the evaluator's own language model) rather than as literal self-authorship. Test: bin response-pairs by the perplexity *difference* between response A and B (computed under the judge model itself — GPT-4/GPT-3.5 perplexities were unobtainable via API, §5, so this analysis excludes the two most bias-exhibiting models from the perplexity mechanism test itself, a real gap), then within each bin compute the winning-judgment rate (probability the lower-perplexity response is picked) — Figure 3 — and compare this curve for (a) an evaluator's own generations vs (b) other models' generations — Figure 4 — and (c) evaluators vs. human raters' own perplexity-conditioned preference curve.

## Concrete results / numbers

- **Definition-4.1 bias scores (Figure 1(b), §4.3):** GPT-4 highest at **0.520**; Vicuna-13B and Koala-13B next, "significant" bias but no exact digit given in the extracted text; GPT-3.5-Turbo lower than GPT-4; Vicuna-7B ≈ 0; oasst-pythia-12B, dolly-v2-12B, stablelm-tuned-alpha-7B negative (reverse bias).
  - GPT-4's 0.520 is derived transparently from a 2×2 confusion count (§4.3, Figure 2): recall on cases where human preferred the GPT-4-generated response = 0.945 ≈ 1852/(108+1852); recall on cases where human preferred the *other* model's response = 0.425 ≈ 118/(160+118). Difference = 0.520. This is a directly checkable, non-black-box number — worth noting as a *methodology* model (report the raw confusion counts behind an aggregate bias score, not just the scalar), relevant to Q12/Q17.
- **Demographic Parity variant (Eq. 4, Table 2, §6):** GPT-4 **0.749**, Vicuna-13B 0.382, GPT-3.5-Turbo 0.191, Koala-13B 0.175, Vicuna-7B 0.052, oasst-pythia-12B 0.006, dolly-v2-12B −0.069, stablelm-tuned-alpha-7B −0.032. This variant ignores ground-truth quality entirely (`P(Y'=1|S=1) − P(Y'=1|S=0)`), so the authors explicitly caveat it "should not be interpreted as an indication of unjust evaluation" (Table 2 caption) — it just measures "how highly each LLM evaluator rates its own outputs," conflating quality with self-preference. This is exactly the ambiguity our benchmark must avoid (Q19: view-quality vs. inspection-outcome contamination is the same structural trap).
- **Perplexity findings (§5, Figures 3–6):**
  - "All models except dolly-v2-12b and stablelm-tuned-alpha-7b demonstrated a clear tendency to assign higher evaluations to responses with lower perplexity" (Fig. 3 caption) — and this tendency is *stronger* in Vicuna-13B, Vicuna-7B, Koala-13B, oasst-pythia-12B than in the human raters' own perplexity-conditioned preference curve.
  - **Figure 4 — the load-bearing chart for the branch's circularity claim**: winning-judgment rate vs. perplexity-difference bin, plotted separately for "self text" (evaluator's own generations) vs. "other-model text" (neither response self-generated). "Across all models, except for dolly-v2-12b and stablelm-tuned-alpha-7b, no significant difference was observed between the judgment rates for their own texts and those generated by other models." — i.e., the *low-perplexity-preference curve is essentially the same whether or not the text is the judge's own output*. No t-test/p-value is reported for this "no significant difference" claim (verified — checked the raw HTML text, no significance statistic appears near Fig. 4); it is a visual/qualitative claim over binned curves, not a statistically tested one. This is a real methodological weakness worth flagging if this paper is cited as load-bearing evidence rather than motivating intuition.
  - Figure 5: average perplexity is lower for responses generated by the evaluator itself, across all models — the authors' proposed *mechanism* linking self-authorship to bias is indirect: self-generation → lower perplexity (trivially, a model's own sampling distribution should look more likely under itself) → lower-perplexity texts get rated higher (Fig. 3) → net self-preference, *without the judge ever needing to detect or use authorship as a signal*.
  - Llama-2/Llama-3 as generators (Figure 6, §5): "significantly higher winning judgment rate with lower perplexity compared to human evaluators" — extends the perplexity-preference finding beyond the original 8-model generator pool, though without Chatbot-Arena human labels for these specific pairs (compared only against human raters' general curve, not model-specific ground truth).
- **Section 6 (Discussion) qualitative case analysis (Figure 1(a), Table 1):** GPT-4's self-preference bias "was often not related to clear factual errors but rather to differences in response styles, such as the handling of specialized domains or the description of premises before answering" — the authors read this as GPT-4 rewarding **adherence to its own predefined output policy/style**, not correctness. This is a second, partially distinct mechanism from pure perplexity for the most capable model, and it is exactly the "shared assumptions, not shared code" framing the branch selected this paper for.

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **Perplexity is computed under the judge model's own next-token distribution** — this presumes API/logprob access to the evaluator, which the authors state was unavailable for GPT-4/GPT-3.5-Turbo (§5), so the mechanistic (perplexity) explanation is *only directly verified* for the open, smaller models (Vicuna, Koala, oasst-pythia, dolly, stablelm) plus Llama-2/3 as generators judged by those open models. For GPT-4 specifically — the model most relevant as a stand-in for "a capable VLM judge" in our setting — the bias is established (0.520/0.749) but its *causal mechanism* is only argued qualitatively (style/policy adherence, §6), not measured via the perplexity pipeline. Anyone citing "it's proven to be perplexity, not authorship" for a GPT-4-class judge is overstating this paper's evidence.
- **The construct being judged is dialogue-response quality via pairwise human preference**, a task with soft, stylistic, largely subjective ground truth (Chatbot Arena crowd labels) — not a task with an objective geometric or physical ground truth. The paper's mechanism (stylistic/policy affinity, perplexity/familiarity) is plausible specifically *because* "quality" for chat responses is entangled with style. Whether the same familiarity mechanism transfers to a VLM judging "is this image an orthogonal, well-framed evidence view of a surface" (a task with a partially closed-form geometric answer, our RQ1) is not tested by this paper at all — it's an analogical transfer, not a demonstrated one.
- **No cross-model bias analysis** (checked explicitly, §5–6): the paper never asks "does GPT-4 favor Vicuna's outputs more than a human would, independent of GPT-4 ever having generated anything itself?" It only measures self-vs-other for the *same* model acting as both generator and judge. This matters for us: our situation (RQ1 judge scoring, e.g., a VLM judging views selected by an orchestrator that may share training lineage / prompting conventions with the judge, without literal shared authorship) is closer to this untested cross-model-familiarity case than to the paper's actual self-authorship case — the paper's title finding (self-preference) is a narrower claim than what our Q3 needs, but its *mechanism* finding (familiarity/perplexity, independent of authorship, Fig. 4) is the part that does generalize to the cross-model case, which is why the branch is right to cite the mechanism, not the headline metric.
- **No construct-validity discussion of "shared training distribution" as a confound** — the paper does not ask whether judge and generator sharing pretraining data/paradigm (e.g., both RLHF'd toward similar stylistic norms) could inflate agreement even absent any authorship or even any literal familiarity signal. This is a gap directly relevant to our Q3: it means this paper is evidence *for* "shared assumptions are the contamination" only in the weak sense of "familiarity survives removal of authorship" — it does not positively demonstrate that "shared assumptions/design philosophy between two independently-coded systems" (our actual worry, e.g., a from-scratch reimplemented metric that still encodes the same geometric heuristics as our RQ1 judge) produces the same bias. That inferential step is ours to make, not the paper's.

## What transfers to our setting

- **The core citable finding for Q3: bias tracks familiarity/perplexity, not literal authorship (Fig. 4, §5, quoted above).** This is directly the branch's designated payload — "reimplemented, not reused" is not a valid defense against judge circularity, because the mechanism the paper identifies (lower perplexity / higher familiarity → higher score) does not require the judge to know or detect that a candidate was self-generated; it only requires the candidate to *look like* what the judge would itself produce. Translated to our RQ1/RQ3 relationship: if the RQ1 view-quality judge (VLM or algorithmic-with-VLM-tuned-thresholds) and the benchmark's success metric share the *same underlying notion* of what a good evidence view looks like (same prompt conventions, same "orthogonal + centered + well-lit" heuristic baked into both independently), the benchmark will over-score views that satisfy that shared notion regardless of whether the metric literally reuses RQ1's code. This directly supports the branch's separation requirement in Q3 (different construct: GT from geometry, judge from image) — geometric GT (face normal through centroid, a closed-form computation with no learned model in the loop) is the only one of the paper's implied "distance from judge" options that fully avoids the mechanism, because it has no perplexity/familiarity substrate at all — there is no "model" to be more or less familiar with a normal-vector computation.
- **The confusion-matrix transparency practice (Fig. 2 → 0.520 derivation, §4.3) is a reusable reporting template for Q17/Q12**: report the raw hit/miss counts behind any aggregate judge-bias or judge-agreement number, not just a summary statistic, so readers can audit whether an apparent "bias" is driven by a handful of cases (here, ns of ~2000 make it robust; our object/trial counts per Q12 will be far smaller, making this transparency practice more important, not less).
- **Two independently-defined bias metrics converging (Equal Opportunity Def 4.1 vs. Demographic Parity Eq. 4, both ranking GPT-4 highest, Vicuna-13B second) is a template for triangulation**: when checking our own benchmark for judge circularity (Q3/Q17), computing bias under more than one formalization and checking they agree is stronger evidence than one metric alone — directly reusable as a validation step for whatever held-out human-labelled slice we build per Q17.
- **The Demographic-Parity caveat ("does not take intrinsic quality into account... should not be interpreted as unjust evaluation," Table 2 caption) is the clearest available formal analogue of our Q19** (view-quality vs. inspection-outcome contamination): a bias/preference score computed without conditioning on ground-truth correctness cannot distinguish "the judge is good at recognizing quality that happens to correlate with self-similarity" from "the judge is rewarding self-similarity regardless of quality." Our Q19 needs the equivalent of the Equal-Opportunity-conditioned metric (Def 4.1, conditioned on `Y=1`, i.e., only cases where an independent quality signal already agrees) as the stronger of the two available formalizations, not the unconditioned parity version.

## What does NOT transfer, and why

- **No benchmark-design content whatsoever** — no metrics for coverage/reconstruction error/view-efficiency, no dataset of 3D objects, no simulation of sensor defects, no notion of a view or an action space at all. This paper is 100% orthogonal to Q1, Q2, Q4–Q16, Q18, Q20–Q22 in terms of *content*; its only relevance to this branch is as the circularity/judge-bias reference for Q3 and secondarily Q17 and Q19. Do not cite it for anything about view planning, coverage, or 3D geometry — it contains none.
- **The task domain (open-ended dialogue quality) has no analytical ground truth**, unlike our primitive-fitting GT (Q2). The paper's entire bias-detection machinery depends on having *human preference labels* as the "true" `Y`, and the whole point is that even against that human ground truth, judges are biased. Our situation is partially better-positioned (geometric GT has no familiarity substrate to be biased about) but also cannot use this paper's specific statistical machinery (which needs paired human labels at Chatbot-Arena scale, ~33k dialogues) — our object/trial counts (Q12) will be orders of magnitude smaller, so the paper's confusion-matrix approach (large-N counts) is not directly replicable; we'd need the small-N equivalents (binomial CIs, as Q12 already specifies) instead.
- **No positive result about mitigation beyond what's already assumed.** The only mitigation the paper proposes is speculative and inline (§6): "when a model exhibits low perplexity on a sample, decreasing the weight assigned to that model's evaluation... may contribute to bias mitigation" — an ensemble-reweighting idea, untested in this paper (no experiment validates it), and not applicable to us since we are not ensembling multiple LLM judges by design currently. No recommendation for held-out human-labelled slices, no calibration procedure, no anonymization protocol beyond position-swap is given — so Q17's fuller control list (multiple independent judges, anonymization, versioned judge, held-out human slice checked for divergence) is **not sourced from this paper**; it must be justified from elsewhere (general LLM-judge literature, e.g. the paper's own citations: Panickssery et al. 2024 "LLM evaluators recognize and favor their own generations," Xu et al. 2024 "Pride and Prejudice," Stureborg et al. 2024 "LLMs are inconsistent and biased evaluators" — worth chasing as follow-up reads if Q17 needs a fuller control-list justification than this paper alone provides).
- **No discussion of what a system-builder should do when the system itself was designed with knowledge of a specific judge model's tendencies** (checked explicitly per the fetch above) — the paper detects and characterizes bias in existing LLM-as-judge deployments; it does not address the "we built our metric/judge with a particular VLM's known behavior in mind" scenario, which is closer to our actual situation (an RQ1 judge and a benchmark metric both designed by the same team, informed by the same understanding of what "good" looks like) than to the paper's studied scenario (an LLM judge encountering arbitrary pre-existing dialogue pairs it may or may not have generated). The branch's application of this paper to Q3 is therefore an **extension by analogy**, not a directly reported finding — worth stating explicitly in any doc that cites this paper for Q3, so the citation isn't overclaimed.

## Answering the specific "circularity" angle (Q3) directly

This paper is the branch's designated reference for exactly one move: **it kills "we reimplemented the metric, so it's independent" as a defense**, because the demonstrated mechanism (Fig. 4: bias tracks perplexity/familiarity, not authorship-detection) means independence of *code provenance* does not imply independence of *judgment*. Two judges (or a judge and a benchmark metric) built by people who share the same idea of what a good output looks like will agree with each other for the same underlying reason self-generated and lower-perplexity-but-not-self-generated text both score well in Fig. 4 — the shared substrate is the notion of "good," not the codebase. For Q3, this pushes the benchmark toward the paper's implicit "different construct" option (as Q3's own framing already names): geometric GT (face-normal-through-centroid, computed with zero learned/judged component, §-analogy: this is the equivalent of using human Chatbot-Arena labels as `Y` rather than another LLM as the source of truth) is the separation move that has no perplexity/familiarity channel at all for the bias mechanism to exploit — it is categorically outside the space this paper shows is contaminated. A benchmark that instead used, e.g., a second VLM (even a differently-trained, independently-implemented one) to score views selected by an RQ1-adjacent VLM judge would remain exposed to exactly the mechanism this paper documents, regardless of how independently the second VLM's prompt/code was written.
