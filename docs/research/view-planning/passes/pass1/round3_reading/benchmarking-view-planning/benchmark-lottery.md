# Dehghani, Tay, Gritsenko, Zhao, Houlsby, Diaz, Metzler & Vinyals — "The Benchmark Lottery" (arXiv:2107.07002, NeurIPS 2021 Datasets & Benchmarks track)

Source: arXiv HTML full text, https://arxiv.org/html/2107.07002 (v1, 14 Jul 2021), fetched and converted directly (not WebFetch-summarized). Full paper read: Abstract, §1–8, Appendices A–G, References skimmed. Local extract: `/tmp/benchmark_lottery.md` (converted from `/tmp/benchmark_lottery.html`).

**Framing note up front**: this is a meta-methodology / position paper about *large public ML benchmark ecosystems* (GLUE/SuperGLUE, VTAB, Long Range Arena, RL Unplugged/ALE, RecSys datasets) — it is not about view planning, 3D geometry, robotics, or VLMs at all, and predates LLM-as-judge concerns (2021). Its value to our branch is entirely structural/methodological: it names failure modes of benchmark design and reuse that generalize past its own domain, and it is the paper our task brief points to for the "we are both benchmark author and submitter" risk. Read accordingly — almost everything below is a *transfer of reasoning pattern*, not a transfer of technique, dataset, or formula.

## Core method / idea

Not a method paper — an empirical + argumentative case that a method's perceived superiority on ML benchmarks depends heavily on factors other than algorithmic merit. Three linked claims, each backed by a case study:

1. **Task selection bias (§3).** Which subset of tasks/datasets a benchmark suite aggregates over materially changes which model looks best. Demonstrated on four real benchmark suites by recomputing aggregate rankings over all (or many) subsets of their component tasks and checking whether the "winner" is stable.
2. **Community bias (§4).** Above and beyond the mechanical effect of task selection, the research *community* enforces a narrower set of "canonical" benchmarks through peer review and citation norms, independent of whether that benchmark best captures the target construct — and this is largely unrelated to benchmark quality (label correctness, construct validity).
3. **Benchmarks are stateful (§5) / rigging the lottery (§6).** Repeated reuse of the same held-out test set across a benchmark's lifetime creates adaptive-overfitting risk (formalized via Dwork et al. 2015's adaptive data analysis bound), and accumulation of task-specific "tricks" advantages later, more-informed entrants. Conversely (§6), when a field has *no* canonical benchmark at all (RecSys, early ALE usage), individual research groups pick metrics/splits/protocols that flatter their own method — "rigging the lottery" — with no external check.

§7 is the prescriptive payoff: guidelines for creating benchmarks, guidelines for using them, guidelines for reviewers (Appendix G checklist), and a section on statistical significance testing for benchmark comparisons (§7.2) that is the most load-bearing part of the paper for our branch.

## Concrete results / numbers

- **SuperGLUE (§3.1.1, Fig. 1, Appendix A–C):** 55 models (from Narang et al. 2021) × 8 tasks (BoolQ, CB, CoPA, MultiRC, ReCoRD, RTE, WiC, WSC). Recomputing Top-k rankings over all C(8,4)=70 four-task subsets: only **6 distinct Top-1 rankings** across those 70 subsets; for Top-3/Top-5, **almost 60 of 70** subset-rankings disagree with each other. Appendix C, Table 2: naming 10 specific architectures (Universal Transformer, Switch, MoE, Adaptive Softmax, vanilla, Funnel, Weighted, etc.), the identity of the "best" model changes with essentially every task subset shown — e.g. task H alone → Universal wins; tasks A,B → Adaptive Softmax wins; all 8 tasks → Switch wins.
- **VTAB (§3.1.2, Fig. 2, Appendix D):** 32 models × 19 tasks in 3 categories (Natural/Specialized/Structured). Kendall rank correlation of the "Structured" category subscore with the full VTAB score ≈ **0.7**; mean Kendall correlation of *individual task* rankings with the full score ≈ **0.60**, including some tasks with *negative* correlation to the aggregate. Appendix D: among 19 single-task subsets there are **12 distinct top-1 "winners"**; among the 171 two-task subsets there are **20 distinct top-1 winners**.
- **Long Range Arena (§3.1.3, Table 1, Appendix E Table 3):** 11 efficient-Transformer models × 6 tasks (ListOps, Text, Retrieval, Image, 2×Pathfinder). The best/2nd/3rd model identity changes across essentially every task-subset combination shown (full 32-row table in Appendix E) — no subset-invariant winner.
- **RL Unplugged (§3.1.4, Fig. 3):** 7 baselines over Atari-2600 (46 games) and DM Control (9 tasks). Mean Kendall rank correlation between per-game/per-task ranking and the aggregated (median for Atari, mean for DM Control) ranking is only **≈0.49** (Atari) and **≈0.54** (DM Control); mean vs. median aggregation of the *same* Atari data also disagree with each other, "which shows standardizing one over another ... comes at the cost of losing some information."
- **GLUE community bias (§4.1):** 7 of 8 GLUE tasks are pairwise-"matching" tasks that structurally favor cross-attention/Transformer-style encoders. The authors manually checked all pretrained-LM papers with >500 citations and found GLUE used for evaluation in **every one** (footnote 3).
- **Label-error effect on ranking (§4, citing Northcutt et al. 2021):** label errors found in test sets of **10 of the most commonly used** CV/NLP/audio datasets; **6% of ImageNet validation-set examples** mislabeled; correcting these errors **changes model ranking**, especially among close performers.
- **Adaptive overfitting bound (§5, Dwork et al. 2015):** if a test set has size N and a new model's designer can see the previous i−1 models' test errors, a boosting attack can push test accuracy as high as Ω(√(i/N)) above true population accuracy; once i≫N, reported test performance is no longer informative about population performance. Counter-evidence cited in the same section: Recht et al. 2019 (ImageNet) found rankings preserved and no significant adaptive overfitting on a fresh test split (score drops explained by test-image difficulty, not overfitting); Roelofs et al. 2019 similarly found no significant adaptive overfitting in Kaggle classification competitions.
- **ALE/Atari protocol divergence (§6.2):** across published papers using the same benchmark, divergent episode-termination rules (game-over vs. life-lost), divergent skip-frame parameter, divergent evaluation cadence (every 2×10⁵ vs. every 10⁶ frames), divergent game-count (49 games in Mnih et al. 2015 vs. 57 in Van Hasselt et al./Wang et al. 2016), divergent stochasticity-injection method, and — flagged as the "final contentious aspect" — Bellemare et al. 2013's original results reported **mean and best-run performance with no variance/standard error**, despite RL being known (Henderson et al. 2018) to have run-to-run variance large enough to produce statistically different distributions from seed alone.
- **§7.2 statistical guidance (no new numbers, but a load-bearing recommendation):** point-estimate comparison (mA vs. mB) is unreliable; should instead compare distributions p(mA), p(mB) via significance testing. For "better on average across datasets/tasks" claims with **few datasets**, cites Demšar (2006)'s recommendation of the **Wilcoxon signed-rank test**, explicitly noting it "can have limited statistical power when the number of datasets in the benchmark is small." For "better on all datasets" claims, cites Dror et al. (2017)'s recommendation of **per-dataset testing with multiple-testing correction**.
- **§7.1 robotics-adjacent citation (Zhang 2021, one sentence only — not detailed in this paper):** proposes that robotic warehouse-picking benchmarks be designed to assess **out-of-box success rate** (first-encounter performance on new items/scenes), **learning speed** (adaptation time to new experience), and **learning potential** (ceiling after mastery), plus guidance on object selection, scene design, execution protocol, and analysis type. This is the single most domain-adjacent pointer in the paper but is not elaborated here — it is a lead to chase directly (Zhang, 2021), not usable content in itself.
- **Appendix G — full reviewer checklist**, verbatim items include: whether "not SOTA" is used as an invalid rejection basis without further rationale; whether reviews credit efficiency/fairness/simplicity alongside accuracy; whether reviews penalize deviation from current trend/hype without justification; whether reviews penalize partial-task underperformance without elaboration; whether reviews assess study of variance sources (multiple splits, multiple seeds); whether statistical-significance analysis is present/requested; whether SOTA claims are ablated against "tricks not tied to the main contribution"; whether requests for more benchmarks account for compute-budget blockers; whether papers deviating from common paradigms are valued on their own terms rather than solely on SOTA.

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **The whole "lottery" metaphor assumes many independent participants** (many research groups, many submitted models, many papers over years) drawing "tickets" against a shared, reused, community-endorsed benchmark. Task-selection bias (§3) and community bias (§4) are inter-actor phenomena — they describe how *many different authors'* incentives interact with a *shared* artifact.
- **§7.2's statistical guidance assumes each "dataset" in a multi-dataset benchmark is itself large-N** (thousands of test examples), so within-dataset noise is not the bottleneck — the small-N concern they name (Demšar's Wilcoxon caveat) is specifically about a **small number of datasets/tasks being aggregated**, not about small sample size within each task.
- **§5's adaptive-overfitting argument assumes a fixed, reused holdout set queried repeatedly across many published attempts** (the "stateful" claim). Their own cited counter-evidence (Recht et al. 2019, Roelofs et al. 2019) suggests this failure mode is empirically smaller than the theoretical bound predicts, at least for large classification benchmarks — the paper explicitly does not resolve whether Dwork's bound or Recht's empirical reassurance is the right prior, and says the answer should be checked "on a case-by-case basis."
- **§6's "rigging the lottery" argument assumes the alternative to a canonical shared benchmark is *worse*, not neutral** — no established benchmark → individual groups (even acting in good faith) select whichever metric/split/protocol makes their own method look best, because there is no external referee. The paper frames this as strictly worse than a flawed-but-shared benchmark ("hinders progress... possibly creating an illusion of progress"), not as a lesser evil.
- **The paper's proposed fixes are almost entirely procedural/institutional** (guidelines, checklists, reviewer education, pre-specified statistical protocols, living/refreshed benchmarks) rather than technical/architectural. It does not propose a technical separation mechanism (e.g., independent judge modality, held-out third-party scorer) for the specific case of a benchmark author who is *also* the sole method author — because in its own domain that case is rare (many participants dilute any one author's self-interest); it is explicitly not designed for a single-team, no-external-participant setting like ours.

## What transfers to our setting

- **Q3 (circularity) — the paper names our exact structural risk but only at the level of *why it's dangerous*, not *how to fix it*.** §6 "Rigging the lottery" is the load-bearing section: a field with no established, external benchmark leads (even without bad intent) to researchers fitting the benchmark/protocol to their own model, because there is no external check. Our situation is a *stricter* version of §6's scenario: not just "no canonical benchmark exists yet" (true for geometry-aware view planning) but "the same team designs the benchmark, the RQ1 judge, the RQ2–4 method, *and* iterates all three together" — i.e. zero of the multi-actor dilution that even §6's flawed RecSys/ALE examples still have (different groups picking different self-serving setups is still N>1 setups being compared implicitly by readers; we would be N=1). This paper is direct textual support for treating Q3 as a first-order risk, but it offers no technical answer — the "different construct / different provenance / different outcome" framing in the Q3 prompt is *not* something this paper proposes; that has to come from elsewhere (later self-preference/LLM-judge literature, out of this paper's scope by ~2+ years).
- **Q12 (statistics with few objects/trials) — §7.2 is directly usable as a checklist, with one adaptation needed.** Their recommendation to (a) treat any reported metric as a sample from a distribution, not a point estimate, (b) use Wilcoxon signed-rank (Demšar 2006) for "better on average across [few] datasets" claims while explicitly flagging its low power at small dataset-count, and (c) use per-dataset testing + multiple-testing correction (Dror et al. 2017) for "better on all datasets" claims — maps directly onto "few object classes, few trials per object" if we treat each **object (or object class)** as their "dataset." The adaptation required: their small-N caveat is about number of *datasets* being aggregated (each individually large-N); our setting is small-N at *both* levels (few objects **and** few trials per object), which their guidance does not itself solve — but it does tell us which named test to reach for once we pick our unit of comparison, and it tells us to pre-specify (before running anything) the number of trial replicates, the sources of variance to randomize, the statistic, and α — a concrete, checkable to-do list for our benchmark design doc.
- **Q5/Q11 (baseline strength, per-stratum reporting) — the task-selection-bias empirical pattern (§3, Figs. 1–3, Appendix D) is a structural warning applicable to our object-set stratification.** Their central empirical move — recompute the aggregate ranking under many different subsets of the evaluation set and check whether the "winner" changes — is exactly the audit we should run on our own object set: if pooling primitive + compound + near-primitive objects into one aggregate score can flip which baseline (uniform sphere vs. geometry-conditioned) "wins," we need Table-2/Appendix-D-style per-stratum breakdowns (per object class), not just a pooled mean, echoed also in the Dutagaci notes' Table 2 finding. This directly reinforces Q11's stratification requirement and argues against ever reporting a single pooled headline number for Q5's baseline comparison.
- **Q9 (ranking changes under injected defects) — the paper's core experimental design template is the right shape for this question, not just an analogy.** Their method throughout (§3.1.1–3.1.4) is: perturb what is measured (subset of tasks) → check if *ranking*, not just score, changes. This is structurally identical to what Q9 asks for defect injection: report method *ranking* under each defect condition (clean / pose-error / dropout / offset), not just absolute scores, and treat a ranking flip as the finding. Their SuperGLUE result (55 models, 8 tasks, still substantial ranking disagreement) is also a caution: even with much larger N than we will have, ranking instability is common — so an *absence* of ranking change in our (much smaller-N) defect study is weak evidence of robustness unless we've pre-specified the effect size we could have detected (ties back to Q12).
- **Q13 (negative results, reportability) — §7.1 and Appendix G give explicit institutional backing for pre-committing to publish a negative result.** The cited EMNLP reviewer policy ("not being SOTA" is not valid grounds for rejection) and the Appendix G checklist item asking whether papers that deviate from the trend are valued on their own terms both directly support Q13's framing: decide and publish the outcome even if the geometry-conditioned action space loses to the uniform sphere. Also relevant: the §7.1 point that top-scoring/leaderboard-climbing entries tend toward complexity/ensembling, and that "rewarding simplicity" should be a deliberate benchmark design choice — a caution against post-hoc complicating our method specifically to beat our own benchmark once we've built it (a subtler variant of the §3 gaming risk, self-directed instead of community-directed).
- **Q18 (intake format for cross-branch evaluation material) — §7.1's "benchmark building blocks" (problem/task definition, datasets, metrics) plus the full Appendix G checklist give a ready-made field list to adapt.** Their three core building blocks (clear problem definition; datasets that capture the right distribution at the right size; shared metrics) map onto Q18's requested intake fields (metric / dataset / GT source / baselines / survives-our-constraints) with one addition worth carrying over explicitly: a **statistical-protocol field** (replicate count, variance sources, significance test, α) per §7.2, since none of the five fields listed in Q18 currently forces that decision to be made per-source.
- **Q20 (release, determinism) — §5's "stateful benchmark" / adaptive-overfitting argument transfers as a warning about our own development loop, not just about external release.** If we iterate our action-space design against the same fixed small object set repeatedly during development, we risk the exact "researcher sees prior errors, tunes accordingly" pattern the paper describes (§5, "researchers... getting inspiration from already published works... who report only the best of the numerous models they evaluated on the test set") — applied intra-team, across our own design iterations rather than across papers. Their partial mitigation (§7.2 "beyond a single train-test split," §7.3 "living benchmarks," periodic refresh, held-out slices not touched during design) argues for reserving some of our survey clouds as a **design-iteration-untouched holdout**, distinct from the objects used while iterating the action space — directly actionable for Q20's "what do we release / does it change what we build" framing.

## What does NOT transfer, and why

- **No content on ground-truth construction (Q2, Q4).** The paper's whole statistical/task-selection argument assumes labels/test sets already exist and focuses on how they're *reused and aggregated*; it has zero discussion of how to construct ground truth for a task that has no existing dataset (our situation for "orthogonal best view on a primitive from a survey cloud"). Nothing here informs GT-angular-error quantification or mesh-vs-no-mesh tradeoffs.
- **No content on simulation, sensor-defect injection, or synthetic-vs-real ground truth (Q4, Q9, Q10).** Entirely absent — this paper's domain (NLP/CV/RL leaderboards) has no analog to injected pose error, depth dropout, or fused-vs-single-view offset. Q9's specific defect magnitudes and Q10's fused-cloud-vs-execution-space framing get no support here beyond the generic "perturb and check ranking" experimental shape noted above.
- **No content on VLM/LLM-as-judge circularity mechanics (Q3, Q17).** The paper predates the LLM-judge era (Jul 2021) by roughly two years; it never discusses a model being used to score another model's output, position-swapping, judge anonymization, or self-preference. The "self-preference literature" referenced in our Q3 prompt is a distinct, later body of work this paper cannot stand in for — do not cite this paper as addressing Q17's judge-control checklist at all.
- **No content on view equivalence / symmetry (Q8), view budgets or stopping (Q6), or fused-cloud vs execution-space scoring (Q10).** All entirely outside this paper's scope — it evaluates static single-shot predictions on fixed test sets, not sequential action selection with a budget or stopping rule.
- **The §7.2 statistical guidance is calibrated for a different small-N regime than ours and needs adaptation, not adoption.** Their "small number of datasets" caveat (Demšar's Wilcoxon test) assumes each individual dataset is itself large (thousands of examples), so the only smallness is at the between-dataset level. Our benchmark is small at **both** levels — few object classes *and* few trials per object — which is a compounded small-N problem their guidance doesn't directly solve; we can borrow the *tests to reach for* but must derive our own required-N calculation (an explicit Q12 gap, not filled by this paper).
- **Community bias (§4) does not apply to an internal benchmark with no external submitters.** The mechanism (peer review pressure, citation norms, "the community doubles down") requires an outside community evaluating and reusing the benchmark across many independent groups; our benchmark, at least initially, has one team as both benchmark designer and sole "submitter" — closer to (and worse than, per the paper's own framing) the no-canonical-benchmark case in §6 than to the over-entrenched-canonical-benchmark case in §4. Don't reach for §4's fixes (broadening community adoption, diversifying canonical status) as if they applied to us; they don't, by construction, until/unless this benchmark is used by others outside the immediate team.
- **The robotics-adjacent citation (Zhang, 2021, §7.1) is a one-sentence pointer, not usable content.** It names relevant axes (out-of-box success rate, learning speed, learning potential, object/scene-design guidance) for robotic warehouse-picking benchmarks specifically, but this paper gives no further detail — treat as a lead for a future round of reading (fetch Zhang 2021 directly), not as evidence already in hand.

## Answering the specific "circularity" angle (Q3)

This is the paper our task brief selected specifically for Q3, so it's worth being precise about what it does and doesn't give us. It **names the risk category** we're in (§6, "rigging the lottery": no external canonical benchmark → the entity defining evaluation protocol has latitude to fit it to their own method, even without ill intent) and **argues the risk is severe, not merely theoretical** (RecSys and ALE case studies show measurable, reproducibility-breaking protocol drift even across *multiple* independent groups — our single-team case removes even that multi-group dilution). But it **does not propose a construct-level separation mechanism**. Its fixes are all procedural: written guidelines for benchmark creation/usage (§7.1), pre-specified statistical protocols (§7.2), reviewer checklists (Appendix G), and periodic "living benchmark" refresh (§7.3) — none of which is the "different construct / different provenance / different outcome" technical framing the Q3 prompt asks us to pick between. Read this paper as the **motivation and stakes** for Q3 (why we must not let the same team's assumptions leak from GT-construction into judge-construction into method-tuning unchecked), and look to other sources (self-preference / LLM-judge literature, likely Branch-3-adjacent) for the **mechanism**. One transferable procedural habit worth lifting directly: **pre-register the evaluation protocol (metric, GT source, statistical test, defect conditions) before iterating the method against it**, mirroring §7.2's "benchmark design accompanied by the recommendation of suitable statistical testing procedures... decided in advance" — this is a concrete, low-cost partial mitigation even without a full construct-separation answer.
