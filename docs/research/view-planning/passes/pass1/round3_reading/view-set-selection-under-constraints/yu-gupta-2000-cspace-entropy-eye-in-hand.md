## Core method/idea

**The problem framing (the reason this paper was selected for the branch).** The thesis's central claim, stated explicitly in Ch.1: for a mobile robot modeled as a point, physical space and configuration space (C-space) coincide, so "where to sense" and "where to move" are the same question. For an eye-in-hand system — sensor rigidly mounted on an articulated arm with non-trivial geometry/kinematics — physical space and C-space are *distinct*, and the two questions decouple in a specific, exploitable way: *"'Where to move' is best posed and answered in configuration space, the natural space for path planning. ... the view planning problem is also appropriately posed in the configuration space of the robot"* (Ch.1, p.10-11, echoing ref. [1]/[2]). This is the paper's founding argument that the branch cites it for: view planning for an eye-in-hand arm is not view planning for a free camera with a reachability filter bolted on afterward — the robot's own C-space is the object being reasoned about, not a downstream constraint on a physical-space answer.

**C-space entropy and the MER criterion (Ch.3).** Model the environment's obstacle distribution as a Poisson point process with density λ. For a configuration q, treat "is q free or in collision" as a binary random variable Q with marginal *void probability*
`p(q) = Pr[q = free] = e^(−λ·vol(A_u(q)))` — Eq. (1)/(3.13ish),
where `A_u(q)` is the portion of the robot's swept volume at configuration q that lies in currently-unknown physical space. Shannon entropy of Q, `H(Q) = p(q)·log(p(q)) + (1−p(q))·log(1−p(q))` (Eq. 2), gives a measure of "ignorance" of q's status. C-space entropy `H(C) = Σ_q H(Q)` sums over all (discretized) configurations, treating them as independent (mutual-entropy terms explicitly dropped for tractability — flagged as an approximation, see Load-bearing assumptions). The next sensing action (view) is chosen to **maximize the expected reduction of C-space entropy** — the MER criterion, equivalently maximal expected information gain, `IG_C(s) = −E[ΔH(C)]`.

**C-zone — the mechanism that ties physical space back to C-space.** For a physical point x, the *C-zone* `X(x) = {q : x ∈ A(q)}` is the set of configurations whose robot volume covers x — "a generalization of inverse kinematics that applies to the entire robot body" (Ch.3, explicit phrase). A point's contribution to expected information gain is a sum over its C-zone, so points that many different configurations' robot volume passes through are weighted more heavily than points only rarely occupied by the robot. This is the closed-form embodiment of the paper's thesis: how much a given physical observation matters is not a property of the physical point alone, it is entangled with the robot's kinematics.

**Closed-form results derived (Ch.3, corroborated/extended in WAFR):**
- Point-FOV sensor, no occlusion: information gain density `igd_q(x) = −λ·log(1−p(q))` for x ∈ A_u(q), 0 otherwise (Eq. 5); summed over C-zone, `ĨGD(x) = Σ_{q∈X_u(x)} −λ·log(1−p(q))` (Eq. 6).
- Beam sensor (finite ray, occlusion constraints): the closed form for expected information gain is **identical in structure** to the no-occlusion point-sensor case, scaled by the length of `A(q) ∩ V_u(s)` along the beam — i.e., in expectation, occlusion behaves "as if it does not matter," and the beam result reduces exactly to the point-FOV result as beam length L → 0.
- [WAFR extension] Generic non-zero-volume FOV (a full range-image cone) with occlusion: Theorem 1 proves `Pr[hitpt ∈ B] = 1 − e^(−λ·vol(B))` for any open set B inside the sensible region — again, as if occlusion does not matter in the expectation. This is presented as a non-obvious, explicitly named surprising result ("not unexpectedly in the light of beam sensor result... It is not entirely unexpected").

## Algorithm: SBIC-PRM and view-node planning (Ch.4)

The overall system, **SBIC-PRM** (Sensor-Based Incremental Construction of Probabilistic Roadmap), interleaves (a) a model-based PRM planner operating only within currently-known free C-space and (b) a view planner that decides the next scan. Two-part view planning (Ch.4.3), directly relevant to the branch's candidate-generation/reachability questions:

1. **Where to scan**: maximize a combined objective `G(x) = w_e·G_e(x) + w_g·G_g(x)`, where `G_e` is the IGDF (exploratory, MER-driven) and `G_g` is a goal-directed term (nonzero only in the unknown region intersecting the robot's volume at the landmark closest to the goal, weighted by fraction `m_g = 0.85` of that landmark's swept volume already known free).
2. **From where to scan (view node)**: candidates are drawn **only from landmarks already in the roadmap's currently-reachable connected component** `R(i)(l0)` — i.e., generation is restricted to the already-known-feasible set from the start, not generated freely and filtered after. Among these, a landmark must satisfy: (i) field of view — the target point x lies in the sensor's FOV from some configuration reachable from that landmark; (ii) visibility — no known obstacle occludes it; (iii) landmark reachability — already true by construction (drawn from `R(i)(l0)`); (iv) view-node reachability — a local (cheap, only-sensor-collision-checked) sub-path exists from the landmark to the fine-tuned viewing configuration. For a 6-DOF arm, the first 3 "gross" DOFs are frozen at the landmark's value and only the last 3 "quasi-internal sensing" DOFs are solved via inverse kinematics to aim at the target — a specific decomposition chosen to make the search tractable, not a general treatment of IK multiplicity.
3. **Fallback when no reachable view node exists for the chosen target** `x_max`: `getWhereToScan()` is invoked again to return the next-best pre-ranked point `x'_max`; at most a 2-step lookahead is used (compute-cost bounded) to decide whether to scan a blocking unknown region first or jump straight to `x'_max`.

This is a real, load-bearing precedent for the branch's fallback-ladder question (BQ8): it **combines** rung (iii) "never leave the feasible set" (candidates generated only from already-reachable roadmap landmarks — functionally a live capability map, since the roadmap only contains proven-feasible configurations) with rung (iv) "defer rather than delete" (fall to next-best target, not discard the objective) — rather than treating the ladder as mutually exclusive alternatives.

## Formal reachability apparatus (Ch.2) — direct relevance to reachability/kinematic-feasibility branch questions

- **s-feasible path**: a path `[q0,q1]` is s-feasible iff, for every point qa along it, the swept robot volume up to qa is a subset of the union of previously-known-free space and previously-sensed volume (Eq. 2.1) — reachability is defined *dynamically*, relative to sensing history, not as a static geometric predicate.
- **s-reachable configuration**: q1 is s-reachable from q0 iff an s-feasible path exists between them — a strictly weaker notion than ordinary (model-based) reachability.
- **Observable physical space**, **explorable configuration**: derived definitions building toward a completeness theory.
- Three named reasons a configuration can be geometrically/kinematically reachable in principle but **not** s-reachable (Ch.2, explicit list): (1) the sense-before-move constraint itself; (2) **self-occlusion** — worked example given: "the robot can never scan the back of itself" (Fig. 2.5), so a goal behind the robot's own body from every s-reachable vantage is permanently unreachable even though a free path exists if the environment were known a priori; (3) a single eye-sensor scan generally cannot resolve a configuration's free/obstacle status in one shot for non-trivial-kinematics robots (unlike whole-body "skin" sensors or point-modeled mobile robots, which the thesis explicitly contrasts, Ch.2 footnote/conjecture).
- **s-completeness**: completeness is redefined relative to s-reachability — an algorithm is s-complete if it finds an s-feasible path to the goal whenever one exists, and correctly reports non-s-reachability otherwise. The thesis states s-completeness of SBIC-PRM as an open conjecture, not proven (Ch.8, Future Work).

## Comparative literature framing (Ch.1.5.3) — direct textual support for the branch's premise

The thesis explicitly separates itself from computer-vision view-planning work on recognition (Kovacic et al.'s feature-vector-clustering view planning; Hutchinson & Kak's ambiguity-function/entropy view planning): *"These works do not include kinematics constraint of the robot (or the mechanism carrying the sensor) and the geometric constraints due to obstacles in the environment. They usually assume that the sensor can move to arbitrary positions and directions and there are no obstacles in the environment."* (Ch.1, p.16). This is the citable primary-source statement for the branch's framing that free-camera view planning and eye-in-hand view planning are different problems by construction, not just by degree.

The thesis also directly critiques the *naive predecessor* selection criterion — Kruse et al.'s "maximize unknown physical volume" (MPV): treating every unknown physical point as equally worth scanning is "obviously incorrect when the effect of the C-zone volume is taken into account... places which are rarely occupied by the robot should be given less priority" (Ch.3.4.3, p.52-53). This is a direct, quotable objection to naive physical-coverage-only objectives — relevant to the branch's coverage-vs-quality framing (BQ1).

## Concrete results and numbers (all checkable against thesis chapter/table)

- **Abstract summary**: on the real 6-DOF SFU testbed (PUMA 560 + wrist-mounted area-scan laser range scanner), the planner reaches goal configurations in **~7–25 scans**, depending on scene complexity.
- **Table 7.2** (real testbed, average scans to reach a fixed goal, vs. exploratory-weight `w_e`; failure = not reaching goal within 30 iterations):
  - `w_e=0.0` → 12.77 scans avg, failure rate 0.10 (2/20)
  - `w_e=0.25` → 14.65 scans avg, failure rate 0.14 (3/21)
  - `w_e=0.50` → 16.33 scans avg, failure rate 0.09 (1/11)
  - `w_e=1.0` → 18.70 scans avg, failure rate 0.09 (1/11)
  Heavier exploration weight costs more scans to reach a *specific* goal, though the thesis notes larger `w_e` occasionally escapes local dead ends.
- **Table 7.1** (run-time breakdown, Pentium II 450MHz, 1000–6000 roadmap landmarks): `scan()` 30–60s, `update()` 12–15s, `roadmap.expand()` 20–33s, `planViewNode()` 5s, `robot.move()` 5–25s (3–7 primitive movements per iteration), `reachGoal()` 3–5s. Total 69–115s/iteration.
- **Planar 2-link simulation, 3-way criterion comparison (Ch.3, Fig. 3.2), after 14 scans from a 14%-known-C-space start**: Random views (RV) → **47%** known C-space; MPV (maximize unknown physical volume) → **33%**; MER → **70%**. Load-bearing negative result: *the naive "maximize new physical coverage" criterion underperforms even random view selection* once C-space structure is accounted for — explicit, numbered warning against treating raw physical/surface coverage as the view-selection objective.
- **[WAFR extension] planar sim, after 5 scans**: RV ≈8% C-space expansion, MPV ≈54%, point-FOV MER ≈73%, generic non-zero-volume-FOV MER (best) ≈82%. To reach ~90% known C-space: generic-FOV MER needs 7 scans, point-FOV MER needs 11, RV needs 33, MPV needs 19.
- **Initial free-region tightness**: assumed initial free cuboid 80×80×220 cm around a PUMA 560 in its most compact pose (67×67 cm footprint) leaves only 6.5 cm clearance per side — noted as *tighter* than a comparable prior setup (Renton et al., 2.34× larger initial free volume) — illustrative of how little slack this class of system tolerates before the sense-before-move constraint bites.

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **Poisson point-process obstacle model** (points, not extended/shaped obstacles) underlies every closed-form expression. Authors explicitly flag this as a simplification (WAFR conclusion) and list a shape-aware Boolean model as unfinished future work — it was never delivered in this line of work as far as these documents show.
- **Mutual entropy terms between configurations are dropped** (configurations treated as independent RVs) purely for tractability; second-order terms would be `O(N²)` in the number of discretized configurations — explicitly flagged as an open question whether this materially affects results (Ch.3 footnote, Ch.8 future work).
- **Static environment, single moving agent.**
- **Objective is "resolve unknown C-space" (motion-planning epistemic uncertainty), not surface coverage, defect visibility, or view quality** — confirmed by direct text search of the full 168-page thesis: **zero** occurrences of "orthogonal," **zero** of "defect," **one** occurrence of "inspection" (a footnote, see below), **one** occurrence of "coverage" (a bibliography entry only, Soucy/Callari/Ferrie, never used in the body text).
- Environment assumed **a priori unknown**; the whole s-feasible/s-reachable apparatus exists to handle the online, incremental-discovery setting.

## What transfers to our setting

- **The founding argument itself, verbatim-quotable**: view planning for an eye-in-hand system must be posed *in* the robot's configuration space, not as physical-space view selection with reachability bolted on as a post-hoc filter (Ch.1, p.10-11 and p.16 quotes above). This is direct textual support for our project's decision not to post-filter geometrically-ideal orthogonal views with IK after the fact, but to treat feasibility as intrinsic to how candidates are even generated.
- **The s-feasible/s-reachable/explorable vocabulary is a precise, reusable formalism for our "unreachable ideal view" problem** (BQ8, BQ10-12) — even though our online sense-before-move constraint doesn't apply (see negative transfer below), the *named failure modes* transfer directly: self-occlusion ("robot can never scan the back of itself," Fig. 2.5, worked example) is exactly our eye-in-hand collision-constraint concern; and the C-zone concept (X(x) = configurations whose robot volume covers x) is a clean formal object for "which configurations would need to be reachable to get any view of this surface point" — useful vocabulary even without adopting the entropy math.
- **The view-node search pipeline (Ch.4.3) is a genuine precedent for combining fallback-ladder rungs** rather than treating them as exclusive: candidates are drawn only from a live, roadmap-encoded feasible set (functionally equivalent to an up-front capability-map filter — never generate an infeasible candidate) *and* on failure the system defers to the next-best target rather than deleting the sensing objective outright. This is concrete precedent for BQ8's headline deliverable: "filter up front" and "defer rather than delete" are not competing answers, they compose.
- **The explicit warning against naive physical-coverage maximization** (MPV underperforming random views, 33% vs 47%, Ch.3 Fig. 3.2) is a citable numeric caution for the branch's coverage-objective question (BQ1): a coverage-only objective can be actively counter-productive once the actual downstream structure (their C-space; our per-region inspection quality) has structure that raw "new area seen" ignores. Directly motivates weighting/quality terms over raw set-cover counting.
- **Self-flagged scope boundary that names our exact task**: a footnote in Ch.3.4.3 states *"Such points may be of interest for other tasks, such as inspection"* — the authors themselves recognize that their C-zone/exploration-priority weighting is not the right criterion for inspection-type tasks and explicitly bracket that case out as future/different work. This both confirms non-transfer of the objective (see below) and is a rare piece of textual evidence that the authors saw the inspection use case as a distinct problem requiring different machinery, twenty-five years before our project.

## What does NOT transfer, and why (negative findings — first-class per task brief)

- **The optimization objective is fundamentally different and does not transfer.** MER/IGD scores a sensing action by how much it reduces ignorance about *which configurations are free vs. in collision* — a motion-planning epistemic objective over an a priori *unknown* environment. Our objective is: given an *already-known* (already-fused-from-survey) geometry, select views maximally orthogonal to and well-framing a given surface region for inspection quality. Confirmed by direct search of the full thesis text: 0 hits for "orthogonal," 0 for "defect," 1 for "inspection" (the bracketing footnote above), 1 for "coverage" (an unrelated bibliography entry, never discussed in body text). There is no analogue anywhere in this paper's machinery for incidence angle, framing, or surface-quality criteria — it is defect- and orthogonality-blind by construction, not by omission.
- **The problem is explicitly online/incremental exploration of an unknown environment; ours is offline selection over an already-surveyed point cloud.** This changes the reachability semantics: the paper's s-reachability constraint (a configuration only counts as reachable if a path exists that never moves through not-yet-sensed space) is a genuinely *stronger*, path-history-dependent notion than plain IK+collision feasibility. Once we already have a full fused survey, that path-dependence collapses — for us "reachable" is just static IK+collision feasibility against known geometry, no sensing-history dependence. This is a real scoping correction for our reachability questions (BQ10-12): we need the paper's *vocabulary and failure-mode taxonomy*, not its full s-feasibility machinery, which solves a harder problem than the one we have.
- **Candidate generation is not "enumerate then select."** The view-node search is a continuous maximization over sensor configuration space (`argmax_s ĨG(s)`), evaluated via search/sampling over a roadmap, not a discrete presented menu. There is no concept of "presenting a discrete action space to a VLM" (RQ4) anywhere in this work — its consumer is a classical incremental planner, not an agent limited to choosing from a small labeled set. This paper is simply silent on the branch's BQ4 (enumerate-then-select vs. derive-directly) question; its algorithm neither validates nor invalidates either pipeline choice for us, because the downstream consumer's constraints are entirely different from a VLM's.
- **No treatment of minimality, submodularity, ILP/FPT feasibility, or coverage-set stopping criteria as a combinatorial selection problem.** MER-driven scanning continues until a hard "known enough" condition or an iteration cap (30 iterations in experiments); there is no proof or discussion of a minimum-set-cover-style termination guarantee. Not usable evidence for BQ2/BQ3/BQ13/BQ14.
- **Poisson point-obstacle model carries no shape/size structure** — the theory represents geometry entirely through marginal free-space probability `p(q)`, abstracting away exactly the primitive-shape information (faces, cylindrical rings) our project needs. This paper offers no primitive-level view-pattern recipe and is genuinely silent on BQ5.
- **The IK-multiplicity handling is narrow and doesn't generalize to our question.** Freezing 3 "gross" DOFs and IK-solving only the last 3 "quasi-internal" DOFs is a specific decomposition for a 6-DOF PUMA + wrist scanner where those DOFs have a natural gross/fine split; it does not address the general "is a view a Cartesian pose or a joint configuration, given up to 8 IK branches" question posed for a UR5e (BQ11) — the paper never confronts multiple IK solutions to the same Cartesian target as a design choice.

## Assessment for the branch

This paper earns its selection as a founding-argument citation, not as a source of transferable machinery. Its single most useful sentence for the branch is explicit and quotable: eye-in-hand view planning must be posed in the robot's configuration space because reachability and observability are structurally entangled there, not composable as separate physical-space-view-selection-then-IK-filter stages (Ch.1, p.10-11; Ch.1.5.3's contrast with recognition-oriented CV view planning, p.16). Its formal reachability vocabulary (s-feasible, s-reachable, C-zone, explorable) and its view-node search pipeline (candidates drawn only from an already-feasible roadmap set, with graceful fallback to the next-best target) are concrete, checkable precedent for the branch's BQ8 fallback-ladder deliverable, showing rungs (iii) and (iv) composing rather than competing in a real implemented system. Everything else — the entropy math, the Poisson obstacle model, the MER criterion, the online-exploration framing — targets a different objective (resolve unknown C-space) for a different problem (unknown-environment motion planning) and does not transfer to our offline, known-geometry, orthogonality/coverage-quality objective; the paper itself flags this gap in a single footnote naming "inspection" as future/different work. The MPV-underperforms-random-views result (33% vs 47%, Ch.3 Fig. 3.2) is the one piece of genuinely portable empirical caution: naive physical-coverage maximization is not merely suboptimal but can be actively worse than doing nothing clever, a useful data point for the branch's coverage-vs-quality framing (BQ1).
