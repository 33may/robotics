## Core method / idea

A **capability map** is an offline-precomputed, robot-base-anchored lookup structure that turns "is this approach direction reachable from this workspace region" from a per-candidate IK call into an O(1) geometric test (angle-to-cone-axis / point-in-cylinder). Construction pipeline (§III):

1. **Discretize position space** (§III-A): the arm's theoretical max workspace is enveloped by a cube of side = 2× arm length centered at the robot base (deliberately overestimates reachable volume), then subdivided into equal subcubes (Fig. 4 caption: 300 mm side, illustrative figure). Into **each** subcube a **reachability sphere** is inscribed (Fig. 5a) — the paper's own numbers for the actual evaluation (Figs. 6–8, Table I) use spheres of **radius 25 mm** (i.e., 50 mm subcubes), not the 300 mm cubes shown in the illustrative Fig. 4 — flagged here as an apparent inconsistency between an illustrative figure and the quantitative evaluation, not resolved in the source text.
2. **Sample direction space per sphere** (§III-C): N=200 points evenly distributed on each sphere via the Saff–Kuijlaars spiral-point algorithm. For each point, a candidate TCP frame is built with z-axis pointing to the sphere center (the approach direction) and x/y tangential; the frame is then swept around its own z-axis in **30° steps** (12 orientations) — **only to check IK existence, not to record which twist works.** If IK succeeds for *any* of the 12 twist samples at a given direction point, that point is marked reachable and the specific successful twist angle is discarded (Sec III-C, explicit: "the z-orientation of the TCP w.r.t. the sphere center is discarded").
3. **Config-space sampling + IK** (§III-B, III-C): the robot's (redundant, 7-DOF) configuration space is randomly sampled (10⁶ samples for the reported experiment, printed as "106" in the OCR'd text due to lost superscript — cross-checked against Table II header "10⁶ samples" and repeated occurrences); each sampled config's TCP position (via forward kinematics) bins it into a subcube. Within each subcube, for the direction/twist grid described above, IK is solved analytically (Craig's method) + redundancy-optimized (Konietschke et al.), **seeded from the randomly-sampled configuration** — the paper notes redundant-arm IK needs a good initial guess, hence the random seeding is not wasted, it's the search restart mechanism.
4. **Reachability index** `D = R/N · 100` (**Eq. 3**, R = valid-IK points found on a sphere, N=200 total) — a single scalar per subcube/sphere, direction-less, summarizing what fraction of sampled approach directions have *some* reachable orientation.
5. **Shape-primitive fitting** to compress each sphere's raw direction-validity pattern into a **cone** (single opening direction, Fig. 11a/12a) or one of two **cylinder types** (t1 = double-cone/"band," t2 = ring/torus-like, Fig. 11b–c) via PCA-optimized axis + swept opening-angle search, scored by a **Shape Fit Error** (SFE, **Eqs. 4–5**): `SFE(a,α) = (u+r)/R · 100` clipped to [0,100], where `u` = falsely-included unreachable points, `r` = wrongly-excluded reachable points, `R` = total reachable points on that sphere. **The final "capability map" is a per-region *mixed* map**: for every subcube, keep whichever of {cone, cylinder-t1, cylinder-t2} has the lowest SFE (§III-F).
6. **Two indices travel with the map**: reachability index D (Eq. 3, how much of the sphere is reachable at all) and SFE (Eq. 5, how well the compact shape approximates that reachable set) — together they let a planner (a) find generally versatile regions, and (b) cheaply test one specific direction against a compact shape instead of against 200 raw IK-derived points.

## Concrete results and numbers

- **Reachability index range**: D ∈ [0, 76] across the DLR Justin right arm's (7-DOF) workspace — i.e., **even the single best-reachability region only has 76% of the 200 sampled approach directions reachable**, not 100% (§III-D). Top-10%-index spheres (D∈[68,76]) lie on "somewhat more than a half a sphere shell" around the arm base at roughly half the arm's length in radius (would be a complete shell absent joint limits); bottom-10%-index spheres (D∈[0,8]) sit on the workspace border (Fig. 8).
- **Structural transition with radius** (§III-E, read off Figs. 9–10, a qualitative visual finding, not a numeric table): near the workspace **border**, reachable directions form a **cone** (one dominant approach direction survives); moving **inward**, cones **open out**, passing through **double-cone** structures, and the innermost/best-reachability spheres show a **ring** structure. This is a genuine, reported "reachability is anisotropic and its anisotropy pattern changes with position" finding, not an assumption.
- **Table I — mean/std SFE (%) per shape family**, radius=25mm spheres, 10⁶ config samples, 30° z-step: spheres (raw/baseline) mean 87.8 (σ21.3); cones mean 12.6 (σ20.2); cylinder-t1 mean 85.9 (σ23.0); cylinder-t2 mean 82.2 (σ30.6); **mixed map mean 7.2 (σ10.7)**; optimal (by construction) 0. Cones alone already do most of the work (outer workspace); cylinders only help in the inner region; the two are complementary and the mixed map's error is roughly half the cone-only error.
- **Table II — computation time** to build each representation for the whole workspace: raw reachability-sphere data (the IK-sampling step) **12.3 h**; homogeneous (single-shape-type) map **1.8 min**; final mixed capability map **6.4 min**; total (sphere + mixed fit) **12.4 h**. **The IK/sampling step, not the shape-fitting step, is the entire cost** — shape fitting is 2–3 orders of magnitude cheaper once raw reachability data exists, and querying the finished map at runtime is a single angle computation per candidate direction (§III-F: "Testing directions against these shape primitives is a lot faster than testing against single inverse kinematics solutions... reduces to one computation of the angle between the shape's axis and the direction").
- **Worked application example** (§V, Fig. 16): an object (bottle) initially sits at the border of the reachable workspace (low D); the robot's **torso is repositioned** to move the high-D region (D∈[68,76]) over the object, giving the grasp planner more valid-approach-direction alternatives to exploit redundancy. This is the paper's own "unreachable → fallback" example, and the fallback mechanism used is **moving the base**, not selecting an alternate nearby view for a fixed base (see "What does NOT transfer" below).
- Grasp-planning integration (§V): an existing grasp planner (Borst et al. 1999) generates directionally-unconstrained candidate grasps; some are "kinematically unreachable" once tested; the capability map is proposed to **pre-filter candidate approach directions before grasp generation** rather than generate-then-discard, to avoid the grasp planner burning cycles on directions that will fail IK anyway.

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **Redundant 7-DOF arm, random configuration-space sampling.** The entire construction method (§III-B/C) leans on having a continuum of null-space configurations to sample from; a 6-DOF non-redundant arm does not have this null space for a fully-specified 6D target pose. (See transfer discussion below — this is not necessarily fatal for us, but it changes the construction algorithm.)
- **Map is anchored to and rigidly moves with the robot arm base** (§VI, explicit): "The map is anchored a[t] the robot arm base and moves with the upper body." It encodes only the arm's own kinematic self-reachability in its own base frame — it is not scene- or obstacle-aware.
- **Collision handling is explicitly unresolved, stated as an open question** (§VI): "The generation process of the capability map or the inverse kinematics needs to discard configurations that cause self-collisions or collision with the table... it remains an open question whether this should be done by or external to the inverse kinematics." The reported map therefore captures **kinematic** reachability only; scene obstacles (for us: the object being inspected, the table, other fixtures) are explicitly out of scope of the base construction and must be layered on separately.
- **Singularities are not discriminated by the capability map** (§VI, explicit): the map's reachability index/shapes say nothing about proximity to a singular configuration; the paper proposes combining the capability map with a *separate* manipulability-based singularity map (§II-A, Fig. 2) if that matters downstream.
- **The compact shape (cone/cylinder) approximation has a nonzero, only partially-quantified error.** Table I's mean SFE=7.2% (σ=10.7) for the mixed map is the closest thing to a measured false-positive/false-negative rate the paper reports, and it is a **distributional statistic across the whole workspace, not a worst-case bound** (σ=10.7 on a mean of 7.2 means individual spheres can have much higher SFE — Fig. 15's histogram shows this spread, not analyzed quantitatively in text beyond "the majority of shapes fit the data well"). The paper's own conclusion (§VI) explicitly flags this as unresolved: **"Some of the shapes fitted to the data still include a few points on the sphere for which no inverse kinematics solution was found. It remains to be evaluated how often these positions are encountered by e.g. grasp planners using the map... "** — i.e., the authors themselves never closed the loop on how costly a capability-map false positive is downstream; it is stated as future work in 2007 and (as far as this reading pass can tell) not resolved in this paper.

## What transfers to our setting

- **The core promise, matching why this document was selected**: an offline-precomputed, per-region continuous score (reachability index D, Eq. 3) plus a compact directional shape (cone/cylinder + SFE, Eqs. 4–5) converts "is this candidate view's approach direction reachable" from an online IK call into an O(1) geometric test. For a **fixed-base** UR5e (per our framing doc: "the arm only for calibration and demo," base stationary), this map only needs to be built **once**, in robot-base frame, and reused across every object/scene — directly answering the branch's "filter up front with a capability map, or post-filter with per-candidate IK" question with a concrete architecture: precompute the map once (offline, hours per Table II, a one-time cost amortized over the project's lifetime), then query it cheaply at runtime for every candidate viewpoint direction generated per object. This is a strong precedent for filtering candidate views **before** presenting them to the VLM, rather than presenting a view and discovering post-hoc that it fails IK.
- **A continuous score for "how reachable is the neighborhood of this ideal-but-maybe-infeasible view"** — directly the mechanism needed for the branch's headline fallback-ladder deliverable (rung ii, "slide the viewpoint inside its admissible region and re-optimize"). The reachability index D and the fitted cone/cylinder's angular margin (distance from a candidate direction to the shape's boundary) are exactly the kind of scalar/geometric quantities that could drive a local search from an unreachable orthogonal direction to the nearest reachable one, without re-running IK at every candidate step.
- **Evidence that reachability is anisotropic and changes character with position** (§III-E, Figs. 9–10: cone near border → ring near center) — directly supports the branch's question "does the reachable set change the shape of the answer or only prune it?" This paper's answer, for a redundant humanoid arm, is unambiguous: near the boundary, only a **narrow cone of approach directions** is reachable at all — i.e., reachability is not a uniform discount factor, it structurally privileges certain approach directions over others depending on where in the workspace the target sits. For our tabletop setup this is a strong prior that the reachable shell will *not* symmetrically shrink every candidate view's viability equally — some approach-direction families (plausibly top-down for objects near the workspace edge, given our own D405-on-UR5e geometry) are more likely to be excluded outright than others, which is exactly the kind of finding the branch's ObjView-Bench question is trying to quantify for our platform.
- **Explicit design lesson on where the expense lives**: Table II shows the *IK-sampling* step (12.3h) dominates over shape-fitting (6.4 min, ~100x cheaper) and over online queries (single angle test). If we build an analogous capability map for the UR5e, budget should go toward the offline sampling density (accuracy of the reachable-direction map), not toward a clever runtime query structure — the query is already cheap once the map exists.
- **A concrete, checkable definition of "approximation error of a compact reachability representation"** (Eqs. 4–5, Table I) that the branch can cite as precedent when arguing whether a false positive from a capability-map filter is tolerable: this paper's own mixed-map mean error is 7.2%, with std 10.7 — i.e., non-trivial per-region variance, and the authors flag (not resolve) the downstream cost question. Any claim we make about capability-map false-positive cost should either measure it directly for our own map (this paper gives no shortcut) or explicitly inherit this paper's caveat rather than assume near-zero error.

## What does NOT transfer, and why

- **No inspection/vision framing whatsoever.** This is a pure kinematics/grasp-planning paper — no notion of "view," "coverage," "orthogonality to a surface," "defect," or "image." It answers "can the arm place its TCP here, facing this direction" for grasping, not "is this a good camera view of a surface region." Nothing here informs RQ1 (judging view quality) or the orthogonality-tolerance question (branch's "maximum admissible deviation from orthogonality" question is completely untouched).
- **Redundant 7-DOF arm construction method does not directly port to a 6-DOF UR5e without modification.** The paper's Monte-Carlo approach (random config-space sample → forward kinematics → bin into subcube) relies on a continuum of null-space solutions to statistically populate each subcube/direction cell; a 6-DOF arm solving a fully-specified 6D pose has **no null space** — IK returns a discrete, finite set (for a UR5e, up to 8 branches) rather than a continuum to sample from. Note, however, a partial mitigating observation (not stated in the paper, an inference from its own construction): because the method *already* discards the TCP's z-twist and tests only position (3D) + approach direction (2D) = 5 task dimensions, a 6-DOF arm retains exactly **one free DOF** (the discarded twist) when solving this 5D under-constrained target — structurally analogous to the "redundant" DOF the paper's own random-sampling step searches over. This suggests an IK-branch-enumeration (rather than random-config-sampling) analogue could be built for a 6-DOF arm, but this is this reader's inference, not a claim in the source, and is untested here.
- **The reported fallback for an unreachable target is "move the mobile base/torso" (§V, Fig. 16), not "pick the nearest reachable alternative view for a fixed base."** Our UR5e base is stationary (framing doc: "Platform is fixed"). This specific application example does not transfer; only the underlying continuous-score mechanism (reachability index, cone/cylinder margin) that *could* support a fixed-base nearest-feasible-view fallback transfers, and that adaptation is not demonstrated anywhere in this paper.
- **Collision avoidance (object hull, gripper+camera envelope, table) is explicitly out of scope and unresolved** (§VI, quoted above). Our setting's "object inflation, gripper+camera hull, table" collision constraints (per framing doc) are exactly the kind of scene-specific obstacle information this paper's capability map does not include by construction — it would need to be intersected with a separate, per-scene collision-free set, which this paper neither provides nor evaluates.
- **z-twist (roll about the approach/boresight axis) is discarded by construction** (§III-C). For eye-in-hand camera views this is often *acceptable* (we may not care about image roll), but it means the capability map alone cannot certify that a *specific* full 6-DOF gripper+camera pose (e.g., one respecting a fixed "up" vector to avoid cable wrap, or one avoiding a specific collision only present at certain twists) is reachable — only that *some* twist at that position+direction is. A capability-map "hit" is therefore a necessary-but-not-sufficient reachability signal for a fully-specified candidate pose in our discrete action space; a final per-candidate IK/collision check is still implied as a second-stage confirmation, which itself bears on the branch's "filter up front vs. post-filter" question — this paper's own architecture is arguably **both**: coarse pre-filter via the map, exact per-pose confirmation via IK, not an either/or.
- **No sensitivity analysis or false-positive-rate quantification usable as a hard number.** As flagged above, the paper states the false-positive question as open, not answered — it cannot be cited as evidence for a specific false-positive rate or cost figure for a capability-map-based filter; only as precedent that the question is real and was already noted, unresolved, in the founding paper of this technique.

## Answers mapped to specific branch questions

- **"Filter up front with a capability map, or post-filter with per-candidate IK?"** — This paper is literally the origin of the "filter up front" architecture, and Table II shows why it's attractive (offline 12.4h once, then O(1) queries). But its own §VI explicitly declines to quantify the false-positive rate or its downstream cost, and its collision-handling gap (§VI) means a capability-map hit is not sufficient for our collision-heavy tabletop setting — so the defensible reading is "coarse pre-filter with the map, then confirm the surviving small candidate set with per-candidate IK+collision," not "map alone, no further check."
- **"What does the field do when the ideal view is unreachable?"** — contributes one instance to the fallback ladder: **"move the base to bring the target into a high-reachability region"** (§V, Fig. 16), which is the mobile/repositionable-base version of the fallback question, explicitly not applicable to our fixed-base UR5e. The paper does *not* demonstrate the fixed-base "slide to nearest reachable view" fallback, but its reachability index/shape-margin scores are the natural continuous signal such a fallback would need.
- **"Does the reachable set change the shape of the answer or only prune it?"** — Yes, changes shape: Figs. 9–10 show approach-direction reachability transitioning from a narrow cone (workspace border) to a ring (workspace interior), a structural, position-dependent anisotropy, not a uniform prune. Directly supports treating unreachable-region reporting as a first-class output rather than assuming uniform coverage loss.
- **"How does IK multiplicity interact with a discrete action space? Is 'a view' a Cartesian pose or a joint configuration?"** — The paper's own representation is deliberately coarser than either: a capability-map "hit" is direction-level (position + approach direction only, twist discarded), explicitly not a full Cartesian pose and not a joint configuration. This is evidence that the field's own reachability abstraction sits *below* full-pose specification — useful precedent if we choose to represent a "view" in our action space as (position region, approach direction) and resolve the remaining DOF (twist, specific IK branch) only at execution time, rather than committing to one fully-specified pose earlier than necessary.
