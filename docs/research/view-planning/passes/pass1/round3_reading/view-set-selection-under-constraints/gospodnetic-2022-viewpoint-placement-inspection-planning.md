## Core idea

The paper is an **empirical comparison of candidate-generation strategies feeding a common greedy set-cover selector**, not a new selection algorithm. It fixes the selection method (next-best-view greedy set cover, Algorithm 1) and varies only the upstream **object space exploration** step, to isolate how much the candidate-generation strategy — independent of the optimizer — determines the final view set's size and quality.

A viewpoint is a **9D vector v = [p, d, u]**, p,d,u ∈ ℝ³: camera position, direction (optical axis), up-vector (Fig. 1, Sect. 4). The pipeline is strictly two-stage: (1) **object space exploration** generates a candidate pool ("generate-and-test": space sampling / vertex sampling / patch sampling, Sect. 4), then (2) **next-best-view optimization** (Sect. 5) builds a binary visibility matrix (candidate × mesh-face, via ray tracing under FOV/DOF/incidence-angle constraints) and greedily selects candidates to cover it — the classic greedy heuristic for **minimum set cover**, stated explicitly: *"The problem at hand is equivalent to a minimum set coverage problem, which is an NP-hard problem"* (Sect. 5, opening). A third mode, **generate-and-use** (Sect. 6), collapses the two stages by picking a random uncovered primitive, placing a viewpoint at focusing distance along its normal, and testing/keeping it immediately — no separate candidate pool. A fourth arm, **manual planning** (Sect. 7), has two human experts use an interactive coverage-visualization tool to place viewpoints by hand, for direct comparison against all automated methods (Sect. 8.3, Fig. 12).

Four requirements (Sect. 3) frame every method's evaluation: **R1** full coverage of the object/region, **R2** adaptive coverage redundancy (more views where the surface is geometrically harder), **R3** minimize inadmissible candidates (visibility-matrix computation is costly, so junk candidates waste ray-tracing budget), **R4** scalable coverage redundancy (the same generator should be tunable to denser/sparser output).

## Concrete results and numbers

- **Test objects** (Sect. 8, Fig. 8): Gear (99,998 faces / 50,000 vertices — freeform teeth + flat + self-occluding tooth gaps), Crankshaft (74,018 faces / 37,011 vertices — elongated, oriented-volume, smooth), Hirth (87,360 faces / 43,680 vertices — central hole + repetitive flat top pattern, like the Gear). "Primitive" in this paper = **mesh triangle face**, not a geometric shape primitive (box/cylinder) — a terminology clash to flag when citing this paper for our primitive-level vocabulary.
- **Camera/inspection constraints used throughout** (Sect. 8): sensor 2448×2050 px, focal length 12.93 mm, **focusing distance 140 mm**, depth-of-field **135–155 mm**, **maximum incidence angle 75°**. These are the actual admissibility thresholds driving every "% coverage" and "#inadmissible" number in the paper.
- **Candidate-set sizes generated**: 292 total candidate sets across the three objects (98 Gear, 107 Hirth, 87 Crankshaft), sweeping sphere-sampling counts (10/100/500/1000), mesh resolutions (4000/1350/675/337 vertices for Gear, similar for others), octree rotation/filter angles (35°/45°/55°/60° rotation, 40°/50°/60°/85° filter), and patch-sampling feature-functional thresholds.
- **Visibility-matrix cost**: ray tracing per candidate averaged **3.81 s**; NBV greedy optimization itself is negligible, **<10 s for a 1000-candidate set**. This is the paper's own evidence that at these instance sizes (hundreds–thousands of candidates × tens of thousands of mesh faces) the *candidate-visibility computation*, not the *selection*, is the bottleneck.
- **Table 1 (no pre-filtering) — best-performing candidate generator per object** (bolded in the paper as lowest #VPC reaching ≥98% coverage, or highest coverage if none reached 98%):
  - Gear: **Octree Filtering, 120 candidates → 34 optimal viewpoints (OVP), 99.68% coverage.**
  - Hirth: several patch-sampling variants cluster near 98–99.8% with 12–15 OVP from 70–1000 candidates; Octree gives 71 candidates → 17 OVP, 98.21%.
  - Crankshaft: **Patch sampling, "no subdivision," 120 candidates → 24 OVP, 99.02% coverage.**
  - Worst performers: Space Sampling variants — e.g. Gear "Min bbox" sphere sampling reaches only **53.33% coverage** from 1000 candidates; Hirth "Min bbox" only **86.76%**; several Space-Sampling rows on Crankshaft never exceed ~34% (Object intersection: 65.98%, others much lower).
- **Table 2 (with the Sect. 5.1 admissibility pre-filter applied before ray tracing)**: rankings are largely stable — *"upon filtering, the best performing viewpoint candidate set stayed predominantly the same"* (Sect. 9.2) — except Octree Filtering on Hirth, whose selected set changed. Filtering mainly matters for methods with high inadmissible rates (Space Sampling), where it destabilizes results; for low-inadmissible-rate methods (Vertex/Patch Sampling) it just cuts computation with no material change to the chosen set.
- **Table 3/4 — average discarded (inadmissible) viewpoint rate per method per object**: Space Sampling is catastrophic (**Gear 61.9%, Hirth 75–77%, Crankshaft 33.3%** average across its sub-variants); Vertex Sampling moderate (**Gear 39.5%, Hirth 45–47%, Crankshaft 26.5%**); Patch Sampling lowest (**Gear 15.9–30.9%, Hirth 11.4–31.9%, Crankshaft 0.08–0.83%**). Per-method breakdown (Table 4): "Direct normal" (vertex sampling oriented along vertex normal) discards only 13.9–28% vs. "Direct bbox center" (oriented toward object centroid) at 29–75.9% — **orienting toward the surface normal, not the object center, is the single biggest lever for admissibility rate** in this paper's data.
- **Stalling threshold (Sect. 5.1)**: NBV optimization is stopped once the next candidate's marginal newly-covered-primitive contribution drops below **2% of the first-added viewpoint's contribution** (the "long-tail effect": *"80% of the coverage will typically be covered within the first 30% of viewpoints"*, Fig. 10 discussion). This deliberately caps coverage at **~98–99%, not 100%** — the residual gap is left for the user to fix later via the manual-planning coverage-visualization tool (Sect. 5.1, Sect. 7).
- **Generate-and-use (Sect. 6, 8.2, Fig. 11)**: converges to **≥90% coverage on all three objects** without any candidate-generation step, but "the introduced technique... shows no other benefits over the generate-and-test approach" (Sect. 9.3) — it does not produce a minimal set, only a usable one, and is positioned purely as a fast-prototyping fallback.
- **Manual vs. automated (Sect. 8.3, 9.4, Fig. 12)** — the paper's first-ever such comparison:
  - Gear: both experts placed **44 viewpoints**; best automated (Patch, exact curvature) used **23**.
  - Hirth: experts placed **21** and **13**; best automated used **12**.
  - Crankshaft: experts placed **34** and **32**; best automated used **23**.
  - Experts consistently use **more** viewpoints than the automated optimum, but their placement is *patterned* — e.g., for the Gear, most viewpoints cluster on one side and are replicated by rotating the object on a turntable (the paper's interface has an explicit "turntable option," Fig. 7, for exactly this reuse pattern) — because it simplifies the physical acquisition rig (fixed camera positions + a rotary table) rather than minimizing view count. Automated sets have no such spatial pattern and would require a full manipulator with path planning to execute (Sect. 9.4, penultimate paragraph): *"the inspection can only be implemented using a manipulator which entails other challenges such as path planning or timing restriction. In that case, the benefit of having a smaller set of viewpoints becomes questionable."*

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **Coverage is the sole selection objective; quality is a binary admissibility gate baked into the visibility matrix, not a weighted score.** Each viewpoint–primitive pair is either visible-and-admissible (satisfies focusing distance + incidence angle ≤ threshold, checked "in the center of the image," Sect. 5.1) or not; the greedy optimizer then maximizes *count* of newly covered primitives with no orthogonality/quality weighting term. There is no per-view quality score analogous to what our RQ1 wants — admissibility is a hard yes/no filter applied before optimization even starts.
- **The optimizer is textbook greedy max-coverage / min-set-cover with no submodularity discussion at all.** The paper never invokes the word "submodular" or discusses approximation guarantees; it simply asserts NP-hardness and applies the standard "repeatedly pick the candidate covering the most currently-uncovered primitives" heuristic (Algorithm 1). Because the objective here is unweighted set coverage (a canonical submodular function), the greedy ln(n)-approximation guarantee technically applies — but this paper never needs to say so because it never adds a quality-weighting term that could break it.
- **Instance sizes are two to three orders of magnitude larger than ours.** Candidate pools run 70–2002; the covered "primitives" are mesh faces numbering in the tens of thousands (74k–100k faces per object). This is why greedy (not ILP) is the assumed-necessary tool here — nothing in the paper argues greedy is necessary at small instance sizes; it simply never considers small instances at all. No exact/ILP baseline is computed anywhere in the paper for comparison.
- **No robot in the loop anywhere.** Despite the selection blurb's premise that this paper spans "generation, greedy selection, and mechanism DOF," the DOF mention (Sect. 4.2.2) is narrow: Octree Filtering candidate generation rotates each vertex-normal-oriented viewpoint candidate within **1 rotation plane for an assumed 4-DOF manipulator** (matching Gronle & Osten's original setup) vs. **2 perpendicular rotation planes for an assumed 6-DOF manipulator** (this paper's own choice, Sect. 4.2.2: *"We assume a 6-DOF manipulator is available and therefore rotates the viewpoints in two perpendicular rotation planes"*). This is the entirety of the paper's "mechanism DOF" treatment — it controls how densely orientations are sampled around each candidate site, not whether any candidate is actually reachable. **There is no IK check, no capability map, no collision checking, and no robot workspace model anywhere in the paper.** "Admissibility" is purely an optical/inspection-constraint property (FOV, DOF, incidence angle), fully decoupled from any manipulator.
- **Objects are clean synthetic CAD meshes**, not scanned/fused point clouds; no defects, no scan noise, no registration error is modeled anywhere.

## What transfers to our setting

- **The two-stage generate-then-select structure is directly validated against the alternative (single-stage generate-and-use) at this paper's own instance scale, and generate-then-select wins on minimality.** This is a real answer to branch Q4: skipping the enumeration stage is not free — the paper's own single-stage "generate-and-use" ablation achieves good coverage (≥90%) but explicitly fails to be minimal and is recommended only for rapid prototyping (Sect. 9.3). This is direct, if indirect, support for keeping enumeration as a first-class stage before selection in our pipeline, since our downstream requirement (RQ4: present a discrete, well-distributed action space to a VLM) needs the enumerated form anyway.
- **The admissibility pre-filter pattern (Sect. 5.1) is a reusable engineering move for our reachability coupling**, even though this paper applies it to optical constraints, not IK: *"By assuming that the viewpoint is admissible only if it satisfies the inspection constraints in the center of the image, it is possible to filter out inadmissible viewpoints before ray tracing takes place. That way the time required to build the visibility matrix is reduced."* This is structurally the same argument for a cheap capability-map pre-filter before expensive per-candidate IK/collision checks (branch Q9) — filter cheaply first, save the expensive step for what survives. The paper's own finding that filtering barely changes the final selected set *when the base inadmissible rate is already low* (Table 2 vs. Table 1 discussion, Sect. 9.2) is a data point that a good candidate generator makes the choice of pre-filter vs. post-filter largely moot — the choice only matters when candidate quality is poor to begin with.
- **"Orient toward the surface normal, not the object centroid" is empirically the dominant lever for admissibility rate** (Table 4: Direct-normal 14–28% discarded vs. Direct-bbox-center 29–76% discarded) — directly supports the framing-doc's orthogonality requirement (Albert's flag) as the single highest-value design constraint to bake into candidate generation, independent of any selection algorithm.
- **Patch Sampling's feature-functional subdivision (normal deviation, Sect. 4.3, Eq. for E_ND) is the closest thing in the literature to a formal, tunable "how much normal deviation before a region needs its own view" rule** (branch Q7). The subdivision recurses until the segment's normal-deviation feature functional falls below a threshold; the paper tests **threshold values 45°, 60°, 90°** for this functional (Sect. 8, "B-spline Patch Sampling"), giving concrete numbers for what "acceptable orthogonality deviation within one view" has been set to in a published, validated pipeline. This is citable precedent for Q5/Q7, sourced further back to Mosbach et al. 2020 ("Feature driven viewpoint placement for model based surface inspection," ref. [17]) which this paper builds on and re-implements.
- **The stalling threshold (2% of first-viewpoint marginal contribution, Sect. 5.1) is a concrete, implementable stopping criterion** for branch Q14, distinct from SCVP/PRVNet/Chvátal — a simple diminishing-marginal-return heuristic that deliberately trades 100% coverage for a much smaller set, with the residual gap surfaced to a human/downstream process rather than closed algorithmically. Directly comparable alternative to cite alongside Chvátal's classic greedy termination.
- **Table 1–4's per-method, per-object view counts are a strong, directly citable dataset for branch Q13** (view counts vs. redundancy) — optimal set sizes here range **12–44** views across three moderately-complex real industrial parts (not primitives), with clear evidence that generator quality (not the greedy selector) determines whether redundancy blows up: Space Sampling generators are the "Bircher-style" redundancy failure case in this paper's own data (up to 77% of candidates discarded, and even the survivors converge to poor final coverage, e.g. **0% coverage** for some Min-bbox / Convex-hull sphere-sampling rows on Hirth in Table 1).
- **Manual-vs-automated comparison (Fig. 12, Sect. 9.4) is a first-class, citable data point against naive "minimal set size = better" framing** (relevant to our RQ2/RQ4 tension between minimality and practical usability): experts use **~1.5–2× more views than the automated optimum**, not out of inefficiency but because patterned, redundant view sets are *easier to execute* on simple hardware (fixed cameras + turntable) — and the paper explicitly flags that automated minimal sets shift the burden onto trajectory planning that a manipulator must now solve (Sect. 9.4: *"the benefit of having a smaller set of viewpoints becomes questionable"* once a full manipulator, not a turntable, is doing the moving). This is a genuine complication for our "small-discrete-set pressure" framing (Q13/Q14 for the VLM budget): minimal isn't free — it can shift cost elsewhere.

## What does NOT transfer, and why (negative findings — first class)

1. **No reachability/IK/capability-map treatment at all (branch Q9–Q12) — despite being selected partly for "mechanism DOF pruning the candidate set."** The only DOF-related mechanism is the Octree Filtering candidate generator's choice of 1 vs. 2 orientation-rotation planes based on an *assumed* 4-DOF or 6-DOF manipulator class (Sect. 4.2.2) — a sampling-density knob, not a feasibility filter. No IK is ever computed, no capability map is built, no collision geometry (robot, gripper, table) appears anywhere, and there is no fallback ladder for unreachable views (Q8, the branch's headline deliverable) — the paper's world has no robot, only a virtual camera and ray tracing against a static mesh. If this paper is cited for the branch's reachability questions, it must be cited as a **structural absence**, not as evidence toward any of the four fallback strategies.
2. **No answer for our regime's instance-size question (Q3).** The paper's candidate pools (70–2002) and "primitive" counts (tens of thousands of mesh faces) are far above our stated scale (tens of candidate views, tens of surface regions). It never considers, tests, or argues against exact ILP solving — greedy is used because at this paper's scale it is the obvious/only practical choice, not because the authors evaluated and rejected exactness. Citing this paper as evidence that greedy heuristics are necessary for our (much smaller) instance would be a scale-mismatch error.
3. **No submodularity discussion under quality weighting (Q2).** The objective here is unweighted coverage count — a clean submodular set-cover function — with admissibility folded into the visibility matrix as a hard binary gate rather than a continuous quality weight. The paper gives no evidence either way about whether adding an orthogonality/quality weighting term (as our Q2 asks) preserves submodularity; it simply never constructs that objective.
4. **Not a source for a per-primitive deterministic recipe like "one orthogonal view per face, a ring per cylinder" (Q5).** Test objects (Gear, Hirth, Crankshaft) are complex freeform/mixed-feature industrial parts, not canonical box/cylinder primitives, and the paper's methods are all generate-and-optimize over the whole mesh — no method here decomposes an object into primitives and applies a closed-form per-primitive rule. The patch-sampling feature-functional subdivision is the closest analogue (curvature-adaptive tiling) but is a continuous recursive-subdivision process over an arbitrary B-spline surface, not a "look up the primitive type, apply its formula" rule.
5. **No closed-form view-count formula (Q6).** FOV, incidence tolerance, and focusing distance/DOF are all present as *fixed input parameters* to the admissibility check, but the paper never combines them into a formula predicting "N views for a face of area A." View count is an emergent output of greedy optimization over a candidate pool, never a computed closed-form quantity.
6. **No universal orthogonality-deviation number, only this paper's own operating points (Q7).** Maximum incidence angle is fixed at **75°** for this paper's own camera/inspection setup (Sect. 8) — a system-specific configured constant, not a derived or generalizable threshold. The patch-sampling normal-deviation thresholds (45°/60°/90°) are subdivision *controls*, swept as a parameter rather than derived as "the" answer; the paper reports final results without stating which of the three threshold values was actually used to produce the headline Table 1/2 numbers for "Normal deviation," so even these are not cleanly citable as a single recommended number.
7. **No survey-cloud / defect-aware input (Q17), no primitive-decomposition fusion (Q18).** All three objects are clean synthetic meshes (tens of thousands of triangle faces from CAD), with zero discussion of scan noise, fused-cloud registration error, or missing/dropout regions. This paper is further evidence *for* the Q17/Q18 gap claims (nobody here plans from a defective self-acquired cloud, and nobody here fuses primitive decomposition with view planning) rather than evidence against them.
8. **Learned selection is entirely absent (Q15).** Selection is deterministic greedy throughout; no learned NBV, no budget predictor, nothing to compare against BENBV/GenNBV-style learned approaches.

## Related-work framing worth reusing (Sect. 2)

The paper's own taxonomy of prior generate-and-test work is a clean three-way split, useful as citation scaffolding for our branch's Q5 (precedent chain): **Space Sampling** (Sakane et al. — uniformly/adaptively tessellated spheres, refs. [7,8]; Tarbox & Gottschlich — densely sampled viewing sphere with fixed distance, ref. [9], producing up to **15,000 candidates from 1000–2500-vertex meshes**; Jing — mesh-dilation-based sampling with a **1/d² attraction force** toward object primitives, ref. [10]), **Vertex Sampling** (Scott — one candidate per mesh vertex after curvature-sensitive resampling and **32× decimation**, ref. [11]; Englot — random primitive-pivoted sampling for underwater inspection path planning, generating **~2500 and ~1300 candidates** from **131,000 and 107,000-vertex meshes**, ref. [12]; Gronle & Osten — octree-based candidate reduction with random-pick-and-remove-neighbors filtering, ref. [13]), and **Patch Sampling** (Sheng et al. — flat-patch-growing + bounding-box viewpoint placement for CMM-style dimensional inspection, refs. [14,15]; Prieto et al. — NURBS-to-voxel sub-surface "view swaths," ref. [16]; Mosbach et al. 2020 — B-spline feature-functional-guided sampling, ref. [17], which this paper directly reimplements and extends as its own Sect. 4.3). Also useful: Tarabanis et al.'s [2] classic **generate-and-test vs. synthesis** dichotomy, and Scott et al.'s [3] 16 view-planning requirements (which this paper's own R1–R4, Sect. 3, explicitly adapts and narrows).
