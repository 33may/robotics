## Zeroth thing to get right: this is a manipulation-benchmark paper, not a view-planning paper

There is no NBV, no view selection, no coverage metric, no reconstruction error, and — critically — **no VLM/LLM anywhere in this 2015 paper**. It was selected for this branch purely as a **robotics-native precedent for the *methodology of releasing a benchmark***: an object set plus a protocol template plus worked example protocols with reported results, motivated by an explicit argument against self-chosen objects. Read it for structure and reporting discipline, not for anything about viewing angles or coverage.

## Core method / idea

**1. The object set (§III.A, Table II, Figs. 1–7).** 73 objects (IDs 1–73), grouped into five categories:
- Food items (1–19): boxed/canned groceries + plastic fruit (complex shapes).
- Kitchen items (20–33): pitcher, bowl, mug, plate, cutlery, skillet, sponge, glass cleaner.
- Tool items (34–53): markers, keys, padlock, hammer, screwdrivers, wrench, wood blocks, clamps (5 sizes), power drill.
- **Shape items (54–68)**: spheres (softball → marble, 8 sizes), cups (10-piece stacking set), foam bricks, dice, washers (7 sizes), rope, chain, a flat clear box + lid — the deliberately-primitive-geometry subset of the set.
- Task items (69–73): box-and-blocks test objects, 9-hole-peg test, toy airplane (assembly), Lego.

Selection criteria stated explicitly (§III.A): **Variety** (shape/size/transparency/deformability/texture, plus a deliberate spread of grasping *difficulty* — "some objects... well approximated by simple geometric shapes and relatively easy for grasp synthesis... other objects have higher shape complexity"), **Use** (span of manipulation purposes — pouring, assembly, tool-use), **Durability**, **Cost** (~$350 total, standard consumer products not custom fabrication), **Portability** (fits a large suitcase, <22 kg airline limit).

**2. The scan/model pipeline (§III.B, Fig. 8–9).** BigBIRD rig: 5 RGBD + 5 high-res RGB cameras on a quarter-circular arc, object on a turntable rotated 3° at a time → 120 orientations → **600 RGBD images + 600 high-res RGB images per object**, fully automated, <5 min/object. Poisson surface reconstruction generates watertight meshes; meshes are reprojected onto each image to auto-generate segmentation masks. **"Poisson reconstruction fails on certain objects with missing depth data; specifically, transparent or reflective regions of objects usually do not register depth data"** (§III.B) — stated as a known, unresolved limitation at publication time, with "better models... using algorithms that take advantage of high-resolution RGB images" deferred to future work. Models are shipped as URDF (ROS/MoveIt) and KinBody (OpenRAVE) files for direct simulation use.

**3. The protocol/benchmark design framework (§IV, Appendix A).** Two-document structure, always paired and cross-referenced by a versioned "Reference No":
- **Protocol** (5 fields): Task Description (highest-level intent, no constraints — e.g. "pouring liquid from a pitcher to a glass"); Setup Description (object list + initial poses, precisely, incl. printable layout sheets); Robot/Hardware/Subject Description (targeted platforms, initial state, *prior information given to the system* — an explicit "what does the system know a priori" field); Procedure (numbered steps for the human experiment-runner); Execution Constraints (rules on *how* the task may be executed, e.g. "must use dominant hand").
- **Benchmark** (3 fields, references a Protocol by its Reference No): Adopted Protocol; Scoring (explicit guidance: **avoid binary success/fail, prefer partial-credit scoring** — §IV.B.2, "it is usually a good practice to avoid binary (success/fail) measures; if possible, the scoring should include the intermediate steps of the task"); To Submit (required outputs: scores, system description, **and mandatorily "detailed comments on... the reasons of the failed attempts and the factors that bring success"**, §IV.B.3).

Both templates carry Reference No/Version, Authors, Institution, Contact — i.e., protocols are versioned, attributable, citable artifacts, explicitly intended to live on arXiv as living documents ("Protocols are hosted on arxiv.org, allowing them to be easily posted, shared, and cited, as well as easily updated as the community gives feedback," §IV).

**4. Six worked example protocol+benchmark pairs (§V, Appendix B/C)**, each with a full template instantiation and reported experimental results — see next section.

## The argument against self-chosen objects (Q18/Q20/Q11 — the reason this paper was selected)

Stated directly in §I/§II: **"The majority of research groups have therefore selected for themselves a set of objects and/or tasks that they believe are representative of the functionality that they would like to achieve/assess. Unfortunately such an approach prevents the analysis of experimental results against a common basis, and therefore makes it difficult to quantitatively interpret the performance of the described approach."**

This is backed by a literature survey of **33 prior object/task sets** (Table I, sorted by year 1969–2014), evaluated on exactly three axes: object count/category, purpose, and — the decisive column — **"Physical objects available?"**. Of 33 entries, the great majority are "No." The paper's diagnosis of *why* existing sets fail as benchmarks (§II, opening paragraph) has three parts, all still relevant as a checklist:
1. Not designed for manipulation → don't cover the needed shape/function variety.
2. No physical properties (mass, dimensions) provided → can't drive realistic simulation.
3. **Objects not accessible to other researchers** → "preventing their use in experimental work" — this is the load-bearing failure mode, not the first two.
Only two prior efforts had physically-available objects: the household objects list [6] (outdated, dead shopping links, no 3D models — so unusable in simulation even where physically obtainable) and the Amazon Picking Challenge shopping list (narrow to bin-picking, no models). YCB's stated differentiator is doing all three simultaneously: wide task/shape coverage + physical properties + guaranteed availability (objects shipped or purchasable) + models.

## Concrete results and numbers (§V, Appendix C)

Six protocol+benchmark pairs, each executed and reported with **narrated failure analysis**, not just a score:

| Protocol | Platform | N | Headline result | Failure narrative |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Pitcher-Mug | HERB | 10 trials | 8/10 scenarios succeeded; scoring = `(M_after−M_before)/(P_before−P_after)`, avg score **0.7884** | Both failures were *planning* failures (CBiRRT couldn't find a return path; pitcher not tilted far enough) — "shows the importance of planning the manipulation task as a whole rather than in segments" |
| Gripper Assessment | Yale Model T vs Model T42 | 4 offsets × ~30 objects | Model T: **122/447**; Model T42: **379/447** (breakdown by round/flat/tool/articulated categories in Appendix C.2) | Explained mechanistically per gripper design (Model T's flexure joints can't stiffen for heavy objects; T42's non-interlacing fingers fail on driller/hammer) — same benchmark used to generate *design* feedback, not just a leaderboard number |
| Table Setting | — | — | Scoring scheme defined (4/2/1 points by placement accuracy) | **No results reported** — protocol published without an executed benchmark run; worth noting as an example of a protocol shipped ahead of its benchmark |
| Block Pick and Place | HERB | 6 trials | avg score **1.83** / perfect-score ceiling of **16.0** explicitly stated | Two catastrophic trials (4, 5: scores −8, −6) from planner failures near workspace edge dragged the mean down; explicitly flagged as such rather than hidden |
| Peg Insertion Learning | PR2, linear-Gaussian controller | 10 trials × 4 durations × 2 perturbation levels (+ no-perturbation) | No-perturbation: up to 10/10 by 3s; **5mm perturbation: 4/10 (plateaus — no further gain from longer execution)**; **10mm perturbation: 0/10, complete failure** | Explicit "cliff" between 5mm (partial) and 10mm (total failure) — a stress-test-at-multiple-magnitudes design |
| Box and Blocks (baseline) | PR2, **uniform-random pick heuristic** | 10 trials × 2 min | Mean 7.3 successful transfers/trial (SD 1.41), mean 10.8 blocks transferred (SD 2.35) | Explicitly framed as establishing "a baseline performance for this test for robotic manipulators" using an intentionally naive strategy |

All six report per-trial tables (not just aggregates) plus mean/SD where N≥6 — no confidence intervals, no significance testing, no comparison-to-null-hypothesis anywhere in the paper.

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **Objectively, physically measurable scoring throughout** — every benchmark's "judge" is a physical measurement (weight scale for pitcher-mug, geometric overlap with a printed target region for table-setting/block-placement, binary grasp-retention observed by a human) or a simple deterministic rule, never a learned model or human subjective rating. This is only possible because the task domain (manipulation success) has cheap, unambiguous physical instrumentation available — an assumption that does **not** hold for "is this image an orthogonal evidence view of a surface," which has no physical instrument to read off a score.
- **Physical object shipping is the mechanism for standardization.** The whole argument (§II) is built on the premise that inaccessibility of the *physical* object is the primary comparability failure — not model/data inaccessibility. This assumption is specific to manipulation research where hardware-in-the-loop execution is unavoidable; for our all-simulation/survey-cloud setting, the equivalent "must ship" artifact is data (survey clouds), not physical objects — the paper's own logic, applied to our setting, supports Q20's "survey clouds, not just objects, must ship."
- **Difficulty stratification is asserted, not measured.** The paper claims a spread of grasping difficulty in the object set (simple-geometry objects vs. complex-shape objects) but never quantifies or validates this claim with a metric — it's a design intention stated in prose, not a checked property of the released set.
- **Community-driven protocol evolution is assumed to happen** (arXiv-hosted, versioned, portal-driven "world records") — the paper explicitly defers the majority of protocol content to future community contribution rather than delivering a complete benchmark suite itself; six example protocols are provided as *seeds*, not as the intended long-term protocol set.

## What transfers to our setting

- **Q18 (intake format) — the strongest, most directly reusable transfer.** The paired Protocol/Benchmark template (Appendix A) is a ready-made schema for a benchmark intake form: versioned reference number, task description separated from setup separated from scoring separated from required-submission-fields. Directly adaptable to Q18's ask (metric / dataset / GT source / baselines / survives-our-constraints per candidate): keep the discipline of (a) a stable Reference No/Version per entry, (b) separating *what the method claims to do* from *how it is scored* from *what must be reported*, and (c) a mandatory "reasons for failure" field baked into the submission requirement, not optional discussion.
- **Q13 (negative results as first-class) — strong, concrete precedent.** Every one of the six worked benchmarks reports and explains its failures in the same breath as its successes (2/10 pitcher-mug planning failures explained mechanistically; Model T's washer/tool failures explained via flexure-joint physics; trials 4–5 of block-placement flagged as catastrophic outliers with cause; 10mm peg perturbation reported as *total* failure, not omitted or softened). Reinforced structurally: the benchmark template's "To Submit" field makes "reasons for failed attempts" a mandatory report component (§IV.B.3), not an optional discussion section. Directly actionable: build the same mandatory field into our own benchmark reporting template.
- **Q12 (statistics at small N) — a genuine small-scale precedent, though a weaker one than the branch needs.** N ranges from 6 to 10 trials per benchmark; every table reports mean **and SD** (Appendix C.4, C.5), not just a point estimate. This is a floor, not a ceiling: **no binomial CIs, no significance testing, no comparison against a null/random baseline statistically** are ever computed — the Box-and-Blocks "baseline" (uniform-random pick heuristic) is reported with its own mean/SD but never statistically compared against the other methods it might contextualize. Take the "always report per-trial data + mean + SD, never just an aggregate" discipline; do **not** take this paper as evidence that mean±SD alone is sufficient — Q12 explicitly asks for more (binomial CIs) and this paper is a negative example on that front.
- **Q11 (object set stratification) — moderate-to-strong transfer of methodology, weak transfer of content.** The five-category structure (food/kitchen/tool/**shape**/task) with an explicit "shape items" bucket of primitives (spheres at 8 sizes, cups/cylinders, foam bricks/boxes, washers) is a real precedent for carving out a primitive-geometry subset inside a broader object set for benchmarking purposes. The *selection criteria checklist* (variety of shape/size/texture/deformability + explicit difficulty spread + durability + cost + portability) is directly reusable as a design checklist for our own object-set curation, even though the actual objects (grocery boxes, tools) have zero relevance to primitive-fit inspection.
- **Q20 (release strategy) — direct transfer of the argument, requires translation of the artifact.** The paper's central thesis — physical availability is the bottleneck for comparability, so ship the thing itself, not just a description of it — translates directly to Q20's framing: if physical-object availability is what made manipulation benchmarks comparable, **survey-cloud availability** (not just object descriptions or CAD models) is the equivalent bottleneck for view-planning benchmarks in a world where the defective, fused-cloud-with-real-artifacts *is* the input, not a clean CAD mesh. Also transfers: version every protocol/benchmark artifact with a Reference No, and host it somewhere citable and updatable (their choice: arXiv + a web portal) — directly actionable practice for whatever benchmark spec we write.
- **Q6 (view budget regimes / multi-condition reporting) — a reusable reporting *shape*, not content.** The Peg Insertion benchmark's execution-duration × perturbation-level matrix (0.5/1/3/5 sec × none/5mm/10mm, Appendix B.5/C.4) is exactly the kind of multi-condition sweep table Q6 asks for when choosing which view-budget regimes to report — report the full grid, not a single operating point, and call out plateau/cliff behavior explicitly (here: no gain from longer execution once accuracy saturates at a perturbation level; total collapse at the next level). Structurally reusable for a view-budget × defect-magnitude reporting grid.
- **Q9 (injected-magnitude sweep, ranking/behavior change) — partial, structural transfer.** Peg Insertion's two perturbation magnitudes (5mm partial degradation → 10mm total failure) is a working example of "sweep an injected magnitude and report the behavior change," even though it's a single-method stress test rather than a ranking-of-methods comparison. Useful as a template for how to *present* a defect-magnitude sweep (a grid, with the failure point named explicitly), not for what magnitudes to choose.
- **Q5 (baseline as an explicit, reproducible heuristic) — direct methodological transfer.** The Box and Blocks benchmark's "baseline" is a literal uniform-random-pick heuristic, specified precisely enough to reproduce (pick a location from a uniform distribution over the box, retry on empty grasp) and run with the same rigor (10 trials, full per-trial table, mean/SD) as the "real" methods. Directly matches Q5's implicit requirement: a baseline is not a footnote, it gets the same reporting treatment as the candidate methods.
- **Q3 (circularity) — an oblique but useful negative-space precedent.** Because every score in this paper is a physical measurement or deterministic geometric-overlap rule, the paper never has to confront judge circularity at all — it sidesteps Q3 entirely by choosing constructs with no learned/judged component. This is weak positive evidence for the branch's own preferred Q3 answer (geometric/analytical GT has no contamination channel), by demonstrating a whole benchmark suite built successfully without ever needing a judge.

## What does NOT transfer, and why

- **Q1, Q2, Q4, Q7, Q8, Q10, Q14, Q15, Q16, Q17, Q19** — essentially zero direct content transfer. There is no view, no camera pose, no coverage, no orthogonality, no VLM, no judge, no symmetry-of-viewpoint concept, no fused-vs-single-view offset, no oracle-selector ablation, no view×outcome grid. This is expected and consistent with why the paper was selected (protocol-design precedent only, not geometric content).
- **Q21/Q22 (metrology standards, photogrammetric network design)** — not discussed; the paper predates and is orthogonal to any measurement-science framing of camera-network quality.
- **The "ship physical objects" mechanism itself does not transfer** — our benchmark's analog artifact is data (PLY survey clouds with real defects, possibly recorded RGB-D sequences), not shippable hardware. The paper's specific solution (mail 73 objects in a suitcase) is inapplicable; only the underlying argument (availability is the bottleneck) transfers, as noted above.
- **The five-field Setup/Robot/Procedure protocol structure is built around *physical, human-executed* experiments** (printable A3 layout sheets placed by hand, "hold for 3 seconds," dominant-hand constraints) — this level of physical-world procedural detail has no equivalent in a simulated/point-cloud benchmark; our intake schema (Q18) needs different fields (e.g., cloud provenance, injected-defect parameters, action-space definition) rather than a literal reuse of "Robot/Hardware/Subject Description."
- **No statistical rigor to borrow beyond mean/SD** — flagged above under Q12; if cited as precedent for our own statistical treatment, it must be cited as a *floor* to exceed (Q12 wants binomial CIs), not a target to match.
- **The Poisson-reconstruction-fails-on-transparent/reflective-surfaces finding is a real scanning-defect data point but is not a *quantified* one** — the paper states the failure mode qualitatively ("usually do not register depth data") with no measured dropout rate, no angular/positional error quantification, and defers a fix to future work. Useful as existence-proof that real scanning defects on standard benchmark objects are a known, unresolved problem even in a mature, well-funded benchmark effort — but it supplies no numbers Q2/Q4/Q9 could use directly.

## Relevance summary

Low-to-moderate relevance for anything geometric or VLM-related (Q1–Q4, Q7–Q10, Q14–Q17, Q19, Q21–Q22 get essentially nothing), but **high, concrete relevance for benchmark-design methodology and reporting discipline** (Q5, Q6, Q11, Q12, Q13, Q18, Q20): a reusable protocol/benchmark intake template (Q18), an explicit and well-argued case for why object/data availability (not just description) is the comparability bottleneck — directly supporting "survey clouds must ship" (Q20) — a working example of small-N mean+SD reporting with mandatory failure-narrative fields (Q12/Q13), and a literal uniform-random-heuristic baseline reported with the same rigor as the real methods (Q5). Best used as a structural/procedural template to borrow from, not as a source of any transferable numbers about view quality.
