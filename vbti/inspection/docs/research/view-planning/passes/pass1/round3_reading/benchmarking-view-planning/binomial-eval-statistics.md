## Core method / idea

1. **Problem framing (§3).** Policy evaluation over an environment distribution 𝒟_env is cast as mean estimation: μ* = E_X~𝒟_env[Y(X)] for a bounded metric M: 𝒳×Π→[0,1] (success/failure, or partial-credit continuous score). Goal: a confidence interval CI=(l,u) satisfying the finite-sample Type-I guarantee P(μ* ∈ CI) ≥ 1−α (Eq. 1) for **any** α and **any** finite n — no distributional assumptions on Y beyond boundedness/measurability. This is a strictly stronger requirement than asymptotic (CLT-based) intervals, which only hold in the n→∞ limit.
2. **The core idea (§4): treat "real-vs-sim" as "gold-label-vs-ML-prediction" and apply Prediction-Powered Inference (PPI, Angelopoulos et al. 2023).** A real2sim function g: 𝒳→𝒳_sim maps a real environment X to its simulated counterpart X̃=g(X); a simulator f: 𝒳_sim→[0,1] scores it. For n+N environments drawn i.i.d. from 𝒟_env, all n+N get simulated (f∘g composed), but only a randomly-chosen subset of n get real trials — this is `D_paired = {(Y_i, f(X̃_i))}_{i=1}^n` (both real and sim outcome known) plus `D_sim = {f(X̃_i)}_{i=n+1}^{n+N}` (sim-only, the "cheap" bulk).
3. **Estimator (Eq. 3, the "Uniform PPI" estimator, §4):**
   `μ_PPI^unif = (1/n)Σ_{i=1}^n (Y_i − f(X̃_i))  [Rectifier]  +  (1/(n+N))Σ_{i=1}^{n+N} f(X̃_i)  [Simulation term]`
   The rectifier term estimates and subtracts the simulator's average bias on the paired subset; the simulation term is the (biased) sim-only mean over all n+N, corrected by the rectifier. This is an unbiased estimator of μ* by construction. A confidence interval is then built not on the raw estimator but on transformed per-sample values Δ_i (Eq. 2) fed into the WSR non-asymptotic mean-estimation procedure (Algorithm 4, §10) — WSR is a betting/martingale method that only needs the a-priori bound [L,U] on the random variable, no distributional family assumption, and gives exact (not just asymptotically valid) coverage.
4. **Two variants:** "SureSim" (uniform allocation, Algorithm 2/Eq. 3) pools all n+N samples into one WSR call; **"SureSim (2-Stage)"** (Eq. 4, Algorithm 3) follows PPI's original construction — separate WSR intervals for the rectifier (at significance δ) and the additional-sim-only data (at α−δ), Minkowski-summed. 2-Stage is proven less efficient in finite samples (§4: "bloating of the rectifier bounds coupled with the small number n of rectifier samples introduces inefficiencies") because it wastes some of its α-budget on a second interval rather than pooling. Best practice for allocating δ vs α−δ: **δ ≈ 0.9α** (§4 footnote 1, restated and justified via a binary-search sub-Gaussian growth-rate argument in §11). A hedged "SureSim-UB" variant unions SureSim (at 3α/4) with the pure-real "Classical" baseline (at α/4) to guard against SureSim underperforming Classical in adversarial cases.
5. **Baseline ("Classical", §4):** the pure-real-only WSR interval with no simulation augmentation at all — this is exactly a modern generalization of a Clopper-Pearson-style exact interval to non-binary bounded metrics, and is the object our branch's Q12 framing is really pointing at.
6. **Validity guarantee (Theorem 1, §4):** SureSim and its variants are proven finite-sample valid (satisfy Eq. 1) as a direct corollary of PPI theory (Angelopoulos et al. 2023) applied to the composed prediction rule f∘g, requiring only that {X_i}_{i=1}^{n+N} are i.i.d. from 𝒟_env. No proof or claim that the estimator is *tight* — tightness is empirical only (§5, §11).
7. **Rejected/discussed-but-not-used alternative: "Control Variate" method (Angelopoulos-adjacent, cited [50]).** Uses empirical correlation between paired real/sim samples for variance reduction, but **is not provably Type-I-error-controlling in finite samples** (§4, "Related Methods"). The authors explicitly refuse to use it as a real-hardware baseline for this reason ("the practitioner cannot know for their problem that the resulting confidence interval... contains the true mean at a specified level of confidence") and only report it in the artificial-data / Sim2Sim setting (§12.3) where it is shown to break coverage in high-ρ, high-N regimes.

## Concrete results / numbers

**Diffusion Policy, Real2Sim (§5.1, Figure 4, Figure 5, Table 1 row 1).**
- Setup: n=60 paired real/sim trials (100 random resamples of 60-of-120 real objects, without replacement — footnote 2), up to N=700 additional sim-only trials from RoboCASA/Objaverse assets, α=0.1 (90% CI), δ=0.9α for 2-Stage.
- Paired real-sim Pearson correlation ρ=0.702 (Table 1) — "on average the correlation on the paired dataset is 0.72" in prose (§5.1; the 0.702 in Table 1 is the more precise averaged-over-100-draws figure).
- At N=700: **SureSim mean interval width = 0.16, vs Classical width = 0.187 → 14.4% width reduction** (§5.1, exact figure directly quoted from text).
- **Hardware-trial savings (Figure 5): SureSim family yields >25% savings** vs Classical, measured by "for each method compute CI at n=60, then search over n for Classical until its CI is as tight" (§5.1 methodology description) — i.e., savings are reported as equivalent-n reduction, not raw width reduction, which is the more decision-relevant framing for our Q12 ("how many fewer trials do we need for the same statistical power").
- Table 1 exact stats for this row: n=60, N=700, ρ=0.702, real mean=0.246, paired-sim mean=0.188, additional-sim mean=0.174, sample real variance σ̂²_Y=0.104, sample rectifier variance σ̂²_{Y−f}=0.054.

**π0, moderate correlation (§5.1, Figure 6, Table 1 row 2).**
- Setup: n=60, N=2100 (RoboCASA Objaverse-filtered), object+initial-condition joint distribution over {1,2,3,4}, α=0.1.
- ρ=0.588 (Table 1)/"0.59 on average" (prose).
- **>20% decrease in real trials on average** (§5.1, cross-referenced to Figure 5). SureSim family converges by N≈500 additional sims — i.e., diminishing returns kick in fast, most of the benefit is captured well before the full N=2100 budget.
- Sanity check to N=50,000 with-replacement resampling (Figure 7) shows the **rectifier interval width is a hard floor** — width does not shrink to zero as N→∞, because it's bounded by the real-simulation gap (rectifier variance), not by simulation sample size. This is the paper's single most important structural finding for anyone considering "just throw more sim trials at it."
- Table 1: real mean=0.825, paired-sim mean=0.820, additional-sim mean=0.772, σ̂²_Y=0.138, σ̂²_{Y−f}=0.090.

**π0, low correlation — the negative result (§5.1, Figure 8, Table 1 row 3).**
- Same n=60, N=2100 setup but initial conditions restricted to {1,2,3} (fewer, presumably harder/more ambiguous conditions).
- **ρ = −0.051 (Table 1)/"≈−0.05" (prose) — essentially zero/slightly negative correlation.**
- **"None of our methods beat Classical."** (§5.1, direct quote) Reason stated explicitly: **"the sample variance on real data is smaller than the sample rectifier variance"** (§5.1, cross-referencing Table 1: σ̂²_Y=0.014 vs σ̂²_{Y−f}=0.029 — rectifier variance is 2× the real variance). Structural explanation (§4, §5.1): PPI only helps when rectifier variance < real variance; when sim is this uninformative, adding sim data adds noise to the rectifier term without a compensating benefit, so the pure-real Classical interval is already optimal.

**π0, Sim2Sim validation (§5.2, Figure 9, Figure 10, Figure 11, Table 1 row 4).**
- Methodology: one lighting configuration is treated as "real" ([0,6,6]), another as "sim" ([0,3,2]) — Figure 10 — to get a known ground-truth mean for coverage validation (not achievable with actual real-world data, since μ* is never known there).
- n=100 paired, N=2000 additional, joint object×{1,2,3,4} distribution, α swept across confidence levels; ρ=0.974 (Table 1, near-perfect correlation as expected since both sides are simulated with only a lighting change).
- 400 held-out samples used to validate coverage (Figure 11). "Our methods always beat the classical baseline irrespective of increasing confidence levels" (§5.1 text describing Figure 9).

**Artificial (fully synthetic, non-physics-sim) data validation (§12, Figures 12–14).**
- Purpose stated explicitly (§12.1): real/sim2sim data never lets you check whether a CI *actually* contains the true mean, because μ* is unknown; artificial i.i.d. draws with a known true mean let the authors directly audit coverage.
- Swept α ∈ {0.1, 0.05, 0.01, 0.005} (WebFetch summary said this; my own read of headings confirms §12.3 covers this sweep but I did not extract the exact coverage numbers from raw text — flagging as **not independently re-verified against raw text**, unlike everything else in this note).
- Control Variate baseline **fails coverage guarantees in high-ρ, high-N regimes** in this synthetic setting (per WebFetch extraction of §12.3/Figure 14 — also not independently re-verified against raw text in this pass; worth a follow-up read of §12.3 in full if this specific claim becomes load-bearing for us).

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **i.i.d. sampling of environments from 𝒟_env** for the full n+N pool (Theorem 1 proof, §4) — this is what licenses Type-I control; if our object/surface-region sampling for a benchmark is not i.i.d. from a well-defined target distribution (e.g., convenience-sampled or curated for interesting failure modes), the validity guarantee does not transfer as stated.
- **Bounded metric on [0,1]** (§3) — both real and sim metrics must be bounded a priori; WSR needs the bound [L,U] specified up front (Algorithm 4). Our RQ1 view-quality score would need a fixed, known range for any WSR-style interval to apply directly.
- **PPI only helps when rectifier variance σ²(Y−f) < real variance σ²(Y)** (§4, §5.1, explicit citation to the asymptotic PPI theory "as shown in the asymptotic setting [17]... a condition that remains necessary in the non-asymptotic regime") — this is the single load-bearing condition determining whether simulation augmentation is worth doing *at all* for a given metric/task/policy combination. It is empirically checkable in advance from a small paired pilot (compute ρ or the two variances) before committing to a large-scale sim campaign.
- **Real-sim pairing requires same-seed execution and per-condition averaging to avoid variance inflation from stochastic policies** (§5.1, "Real-Simulation Evaluation Gap" paragraph) — the authors explicitly had to (1) synchronize random seeds real vs. sim, and (2) sample **20 initial conditions from a 2cm×2cm box around the real (x,y) initial condition and average**, purely to make the paired correlation usable. Without this, a single discrete real trial paired with a single discrete sim trial for a stochastic policy is "unlikely to see high correlation" (their words) — this is a direct, load-bearing engineering finding, not a footnote.
- **The paper explicitly treats simulation-vs-real gap magnitude as an empirical, task-and-metric-dependent quantity**, not a fixed constant — moving from initial-condition set {1,2,3,4} to {1,2,3} for the same policy/task flipped ρ from 0.59 to −0.05 (§5.1). This means correlation/benefit is not a property of "the simulator" in the abstract, it's a property of the specific (policy, task, metric, condition-set) tuple being evaluated, and must be re-checked per benchmark configuration, not assumed once.

## What transfers to our setting

1. **The core statistical apparatus (WSR non-asymptotic CIs, generalizing Clopper-Pearson to bounded non-binary metrics) directly answers Q12's "commit to binomial confidence intervals" ask** — but the paper's own tool (WSR, §10 Algorithm 4, citing Waudby-Smith & Ramdas) is strictly more general than Clopper-Pearson: it works for our partial-credit view-quality scores (e.g., an angular-deviation-derived [0,1] score) without needing to binarize to pass/fail first. If our RQ1 judge outputs a bounded continuous or discrete-partial score (as SureSim's own partial-success scores 0/0.25/0.5/0.75/1 do, §5.1 "Evaluation Metrics"), WSR is a better-fitting tool than literal Clopper-Pearson, which is Bernoulli-only. Concretely actionable: adopt WSR-style intervals for our per-object/per-method success or view-quality rates rather than plain Clopper-Pearson once our metric goes beyond binary success/fail.
2. **The "savings" framing (Figure 5 methodology: compute a competitor's CI at fixed n, then binary-search the n needed for Classical to match it) is a directly reusable evaluation-cost metric for us** — instead of reporting only "our method has a tighter CI," report "our method needs N× fewer real UR5e trials to match Classical's CI at fixed α." This is a much more decision-relevant number for a resource-constrained lab (Q12) than raw width.
3. **The negative-result structure (π0 low-ρ case, §5.1, Figure 8, Table 1 row 3) is a template for Q13 ("what does a negative result look like, and is it reportable").** SureSim's authors pre-specified the diagnostic (compare σ̂²_{Y−f} vs σ̂²_Y) and reported "none of our methods beat Classical" plainly, with the mechanism named. For our benchmark: if a geometry-conditioned action space's simulated view-quality score does not correlate with real-execution outcome (analogous to their ρ≈−0.05 case), the equivalent move is not to suppress the result but to report the variance comparison and conclude that whatever "cheap proxy" we were hoping to lean on (sim-only or PLY-only judging) cannot substitute for real trials for that particular configuration — directly reusable as a pre-registered stopping rule for when to trust our own PLY-based analytical judge vs. requiring real-robot verification.
4. **The rectifier-floor finding (Figure 7, sim to N=50,000) is the correct mental model for anything in our pipeline that tries to "scale simulation to compensate for few real trials"**: past a fairly small N (they converge by N≈500 in the moderate-ρ case), adding more cheap sim/PLY-based samples stops helping — the floor is set by how well the cheap proxy (sim, or in our case a PLY-derived analytical score) predicts the real/execution outcome, not by sample count. This bears directly on our Q2/Q4 concern about whether a PLY-fit-based GT (survey cloud, defective) can stand in for a "true" mesh GT — the paper's finding says: however much synthetic/PLY-based evaluation we generate, our effective precision is capped by the real2sim (or PLY-to-execution) correlation, not by how much we generate.
5. **Real2sim gap mitigation tactics (§5.1: same-seed pairing, small-box multi-sample averaging around the nominal condition) are directly applicable engineering practices** if we ever build a simulated (e.g., PyBullet/Isaac) counterpart of our UR5e+D405 setup to pre-screen candidate view-selection methods before spending real trials — the paper is explicit that naive 1:1 discrete pairing under policy stochasticity fails to produce usable correlation, and describes exactly the averaging fix that worked for them.
6. **The correlation-based go/no-go check (σ²(Y−f) vs σ²(Y)) is a cheap, principled pre-registration test our benchmark can run on a small pilot before committing to any "cheap proxy scales, real trials don't have to" claim** — directly serves Q12's "fix the effect size we need to detect, then derive required objects × trials," since this paper gives the exact quantity (rectifier variance vs. outcome variance) that determines whether more pilot trials are worth collecting at all.

## What does NOT transfer, and why

- **No content on view planning, coverage, geometry, or GT construction whatsoever** — checked explicitly across all read sections. This paper is 100% orthogonal to Q1–Q4, Q7, Q8, Q11, Q14–Q16, Q18–Q22 in terms of *content*; it is purely a statistical-inference-for-evaluation paper about combining cheap (simulated) and expensive (real) *policy rollout* trials, not about scoring the quality of a captured view or an evidence image at all. Do not cite it for anything about view quality, action-space design, or primitive geometry.
- **The metric being estimated is task success/partial-completion of a manipulation policy, not view quality or defect-detection accuracy** — the PPI formalism transfers as a *statistical technique* (item 1 above) but the specific numbers (ρ=0.59–0.97 for correlated settings, 14.4%/20–25% savings) are policy-evaluation-specific and should not be quoted as if they predict what correlation or savings our PLY-vs-real-execution view-quality proxy would achieve. Our (object, view-selection-method) pairing is a structurally different experiment from their (object, policy-rollout) pairing — different source of stochasticity, different failure modes, no reason to expect the same ρ.
- **Their real2sim pipeline requires a physics-based simulator (ManiSkill3) with a hand-calibrated robot/camera/table/background replica per §5.1 "Experimental Setup," plus per-object 3D reconstruction (Meshy, single-image-to-mesh) for ~120 real objects** — none of this exists in our current toolchain (we work directly with survey-cloud PLYs, not calibrated physics sims with matched cameras/lighting). If we wanted to use SureSim's PPI machinery to combine "cheap proxy" and "expensive real" evaluations of view-selection methods, we would need an equivalent real2sim (or PLY2real) bridge — a nontrivial infrastructure investment the paper does not solve for us, only demonstrates is *possible* to build.
- **The 20–25% savings and 14.4% width reduction numbers are averaged over 100 random resamples of a 60-of-120 object bank (Diffusion Policy) or fixed n=60 for π0 (§5.1, footnote 2, Table 1)** — these are not single-run point estimates; if we ever compare to this paper's numbers, we must reproduce the same "average over repeated random subsamples" protocol, not report a single-draw savings number, or the comparison is not apples-to-apples.
- **No treatment of judge/VLM circularity, self-preference, or scoring-provenance separation (Q3)** — irrelevant to that question entirely; this paper's "ground truth" (Y) is always a physically-executed real robot trial scored by a human/scripted rubric (§5.1 "Evaluation Metrics": 0/0.25/0.5/0.75/1 for real, 0/0.5/1 for sim, both rubric-based not VLM-judged), so it has nothing to say about VLM-as-judge contamination.
- **No treatment of symmetry/view-equivalence (Q8), stopping-regimes (Q6), or VLM spatial failure modes (Q16)** — checked explicitly, absent from all sections read.

## Direct answers to the branch questions this paper actually serves

**Q12 (statistical treatment, few objects/trials) — primary target.** This paper is the clearest available source for "what confidence-interval machinery to commit to" specifically because it (a) generalizes Clopper-Pearson-style exact coverage to non-binary bounded metrics via WSR, (b) gives a decision-relevant "trials saved" framing rather than raw width, and (c) gives an explicit, checkable pre-condition (rectifier variance < outcome variance) for whether augmenting real trials with a cheap correlated proxy (simulation for them; potentially PLY-derived analytical scoring for us) is worth doing at all before committing budget to building that proxy. It does **not**, however, give us n/α values to directly reuse — our object/trial counts, metric bounds, and correlation structure are all different from theirs and would need to be estimated from our own pilot data using the same WSR/rectifier-variance diagnostic they describe.

**Q13 (negative results, in advance).** Directly modeled by their π0 low-ρ case (§5.1, Figure 8): pre-specify the diagnostic (compare rectifier vs. outcome variance / correlation), then report "method X does not beat baseline Y" plainly with the mechanism, rather than suppressing it. Reusable as the exact shape our own "geometry-conditioned action space loses to uniform sphere" pre-registration (branch Q13) should take.

**Q9/Q10 (sensor defects; fused-vs-single-view offset as condition vs. subject) — partial, by analogy only.** The paper's "real-simulation evaluation gap" (§5.1) is structurally the same *kind* of problem as our fused-cloud-vs-execution-space offset: a systematic, condition-dependent gap between a cheap/available signal and the real outcome that must be measured (not assumed away) per configuration. Their finding that gap magnitude is condition-set-dependent (ρ flips from 0.59 to −0.05 just by changing which initial conditions are included, §5.1) directly supports treating our ~4cm fused/single-view offset as an empirically-measured-per-condition benchmark variable (their approach) rather than a fixed constant — but the paper does not inject or characterize sensor defects (pose error, dropout) in the sense Q9 asks about; this is an analogy about methodology (measure the gap directly, don't assume it), not a source of applicable numbers.

**Q20 (release/determinism) — weak/indirect.** The paper's artificial-data validation approach (§12, synthetic ground truth specifically because real μ* is never knowable) is a relevant precedent for our own Q4/Q20 concern about needing a mesh-level ground truth to validate any benchmark metric at all — but it is not about dataset release practices per se.
