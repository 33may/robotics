## Core idea

This is exactly the RQ3-relevant document the assignment says it is: a taxonomy + head-to-head benchmark of **viewpoint-candidate-set generation** methods for model-based inspection planning, split into three families (Sect. 2, restated with full method detail in Sect. 4):

1. **Space sampling** — candidates generated in the space *around* the object, independent of geometry (sphere sampling), with geometry entering only *passively* afterward to reposition/reject candidates (Sect. 4.1).
2. **Vertex sampling** — candidates generated *at* mesh vertices, using the vertex + its normal (Sect. 4.2).
3. **Patch sampling** — candidates generated per *subdivided surface region* (B-spline patch, from the companion paper), sized adaptively by a geometric feature functional (curvature, flatness, area…) (Sect. 4.3).

It also documents two more approaches for completeness: **generate-and-use** (no candidate set at all — Sect. 6), and **manual/expert planning** via an interactive tool (Sect. 7). All are then run through a common downstream optimizer (greedy next-best-view / set-cover, Sect. 5) and measured on the *same* coverage metric across three CAD test objects, which is the direct empirical comparison our benchmark question (branch Q18/Q20) asks whether anyone has done.

A **viewpoint** is defined once, globally, as a **9D continuous pose vector** (Sect. 4, Fig. 1):
`v = [p, d, u]`, `p, d, u ∈ ℝ³` — camera position, optical-axis direction, up-vector. There is no symbolic/discrete "address" layer anywhere in the pipeline: candidates are poses from the start, and the only "index" a viewpoint ever gets is its position in whatever list the generation method or the greedy NBV selection produces (Algorithm 1). This is a first-class negative finding for the addressing branch (see below).

## Method detail, by family

### 4.1 Space sampling
- **4.1.1 Fixed-distance sphere sampling**: uniform sphere sampling around the object's bounding-box center; camera placed at each sample point pointed at the object center; up-vector from a fixed helper vector cross product; radius set from bbox extent (max/avg/min — Fig. 2a–c). *No geometry involvement at generation time at all.*
- **4.1.2 Constraint-aware sphere sampling**: same sphere start, but viewpoints are **repositioned** by ray-casting along the direction vector to the (a) object surface, (b) convex hull, or (c) mixed — moving the focal point onto the intersection so the focusing-distance constraint is met (Fig. 2d–f); optionally also **reoriented** to `d = −n` (surface normal) to satisfy incidence-angle constraints (Fig. 2g–i, "norm." variants). If the optical axis never intersects the object, "the viewpoint remains unchanged" (p.5) — i.e., it is left as a very likely inadmissible candidate, not repaired.

### 4.2 Vertex sampling
- **4.2.1 Direct vertex sampling**: one candidate per mesh vertex, camera displaced by a fixed focusing distance either toward the object centroid or along the negated vertex normal (Fig. 3). Fully dependent on mesh resolution — decimating the mesh (they use MeshLab's Quadric Edge Collapse, ref. [19]) can silently delete vertices from flat regions, producing *gaps with zero candidates there* even though the region needs coverage (Fig. 3a,c; discussed Sect. 9.1.2).
- **4.2.2 Octree filtering** (from Gronle & Osten [13]): generate one viewpoint per vertex oriented along −normal, then create rotated duplicates (up to 90° from original, rotated in **two** perpendicular planes for a 6-DoF arm — Fig. 4a), insert all into an octree subdivided to a volume/viewpoint-count threshold, then **prune within each leaf**: pick one candidate at random, delete any neighbor in the same leaf whose orientation differs by less than a threshold angle φ (Fig. 4b). This is a concrete, literal instance of "similarity-merging pruning" keyed on a spatial-cell adjacency (octree leaf = neighborhood) — worth carrying to Branch-2 pruning-mechanism inventory.

### 4.3 Patch sampling (B-spline / feature functional; full derivation in companion paper, summarized here)
- Convert mesh → quad mesh → C⁰-continuous network of B-spline surfaces (Eck & Hoppe [7] Voronoi/Delaunay-based quad remeshing).
- Recursively subdivide each B-spline's parameter domain into quadrants until a chosen **feature functional** `E(S,Ω)` evaluated on the segment drops below a threshold `t` (this is the analogue of a quadtree in parameter space, *not* on the mesh or on a sphere).
- One viewpoint candidate per leaf segment: pivot point = surface evaluated at the segment's parameter-domain center; camera position = pivot + `d · n(u_c, v_c)` along the analytic surface normal — i.e., **closed-form, deterministic "face-orthogonal" placement** given the segment. This is precisely the mechanism branch Q2 asks about ("orthogonal view of face 3 as a native address, not a nearest-neighbour lookup"): here the *segment itself* (a leaf of the recursive subdivision) is the address, and the orthogonal pose is a closed-form function of it. **Caveat: this closed-form step is stated, with the equation, in the companion Mosbach et al. paper (their eq. 6, Fig. 5), not spelled out again in this survey — the survey (Sect. 4.3) only restates the four feature functionals used and cites [17] for the placement rule.**
- Feature functionals tested (Fig. 5a–e in this survey): **curvature** `E_κ = ∬(κ1²+κ2²)‖∂uS×∂vS‖ du dv` (integral of squared principal curvatures — zero iff flat); **thin-plate energy** `E_TP = ∬‖∂uuS‖² + 2‖∂uvS‖² + ‖∂vvS‖² du dv` (cheaper curvature proxy, can be nonzero on flat regions); **surface area** `E_A = ∬‖∂uS×∂vS‖ du dv` (near-uniform sampling, useful combined with curvature to bound flat-region redundancy); **normal deviation** `E_ND = max_{(u,v)∈Ω} cos⁻¹(n_avg(S,Ω)·n(u,v))` (max angular deviation from the patch's average normal); and a **no-subdivision** baseline (one viewpoint per *original*, unsubdivided B-spline patch — Fig. 5e).
- **Input requirement**: needs a B-spline surface network, not a raw triangle mesh or point cloud directly. Building that network from a triangulated mesh is itself a nontrivial preprocessing pipeline (quad remeshing + least-squares fit + thin-plate fairing term, `(1-λ)E_LS + λE_TP → min`), and per the companion paper's own caveat, **quality degrades with scan noise**: "the quality of the B-spline model strongly decreases when exhibiting significant noise or incorrect topology — problems that can arise when using low-resolution 3D scanning technology." This survey does not re-test that claim on noisy data itself — all three test objects (Gear, Hirth, Crankshaft) are clean synthetic CAD meshes (Fig. 8), **not** scanned/fused point clouds with defects. This is a direct **non-transfer** flag for our survey-cloud setting.

### Requirements used to judge all methods (Sect. 3)
- R1 Provide full coverage
- R2 Exhibit adaptive coverage redundancy (more candidates where the object is more complex)
- R3 Minimize the amount of inadmissible viewpoints
- R4 Have scalable coverage redundancy (same method, different candidate densities)

### Downstream selection: greedy next-best-view (Sect. 5, Algorithm 1)
- Build an `n_F × n_vpc` binary **visibility matrix** `A` via ray tracing per candidate (camera settings: sensor, focal length, focusing distance, DOF, max incidence angle).
- Problem is exactly **Set Cover**, NP-hard (Eq. 11 restated informally in Sect. 5), solved greedily: repeatedly cover the least-observed primitive with the candidate that sees the most primitives.
- **Modifications (Sect. 5.1)**: (a) pre-filter candidates that fail focusing-distance/incidence-angle constraints *in the image center* before ray tracing, to cut visibility-matrix cost; (b) **stalling threshold** = 2% of best-viewpoint coverage contribution — stop NBV once the next candidate would add less than that, trading 100% coverage for practicality (typically yields 98–99%, not 100%; remaining gaps must be found/fixed manually via the coverage-visualization tool of Sect. 7).

### Generate-and-use (Sect. 6)
No candidate set at all: repeatedly pick a random *uncovered* mesh primitive, place a viewpoint at focusing distance along its normal, ray-trace it; if admissible, keep it and mark newly covered primitives, else discard the viewpoint **and drop that primitive from further consideration** (i.e., a failed pivot doesn't get retried). Converges to ≥90% coverage on all three objects (Fig. 11) but "shows no other benefits over the generate-and-test approach" beyond being parameter-free (Sect. 9.3) — no minimality, no control over final candidate count/pattern.

### Manual planning (Sect. 7)
Interactive tool distinguishes **anchored viewpoints** (focal point constrained to lie *on* the object surface) vs **floating viewpoints** (focal point on the convex hull, not touching the surface) — the paper's only explicit binary taxonomy of "what a viewpoint's target actually is." A **turntable option** lets an expert select viewpoints and replicate them under a fixed rotation in world coordinates (Fig. 7) — this is the one place in the paper where a genuinely *relational* operation between viewpoints appears ("this viewpoint, rotated by Δθ, several times"), and it's a manual/expert operation, not something the automated methods produce.

## Concrete results / numbers (checkable against Tables 1–7, Figs. 8–12)

**Test objects** (Fig. 8): Gear — 99,998 faces / 50,000 vertices; Crankshaft — 74,018 faces / 37,011 vertices; Hirth — 87,360 faces / 43,680 vertices. All are clean CAD meshes, not scans.

**Camera model used for ray tracing** (Sect. 8, matches a real camera): sensor 2448×2050 px, focal length 12.93 (mm), focusing distance 140 mm, DOF 135–155 mm, max incidence angle 75°.

**Candidate-set scale**: sphere sampling tested at 10/100/500/1000 candidates; vertex sampling tied to mesh resolution (Gear reduced to 4000/1350/675/337 vertices, etc., via Quadric Edge Collapse); patch sampling threshold set as fraction of average feature-functional value (1, 0.75, 0.5 for area/curvature/thin-plate; 45°/60°/90° for normal deviation). In total: 292 candidate sets generated and NBV-optimized (98 Gear, 107 Hirth, 87 Crankshaft).

**Headline coverage/optimal-viewpoint-count comparison (Table 1, full candidate set, no pre-filtering; Table 2, same but with each method's own redundancy filter applied first)**:
- **Space sampling is uniformly the worst family.** E.g. Gear: fixed-distance sphere sampling variants top out at 48.7–99.05% coverage but with the highest **rejection rates** — Table 3: average discarded (inadmissible) candidates per model = **61.9% (Gear), 75.3–77.0% (Hirth), 26.5–33.3% (Crankshaft)** for space sampling, vs. **15.9–26.6% (patch sampling)** and **39.5–46.8% (vertex sampling)**. Sect. 9.1.1 states plainly: *"Space Sampling methods show no benefits and their use is not recommended."* — **This is the paper's uniform-sphere baseline, i.e. structurally what our current `{h,v}` viewsphere is, and it is the family the paper singles out as worst.**
- **Vertex and patch sampling are "both good approaches."** Best performers per object/method (bold cells in Table 1/2, my best reading of the OCR'd tables — verify against source PDF if exact digits matter for a claim): Gear best ≈ 33 optimal viewpoints at ~99% coverage (exact-curvature patch sampling); Hirth best ≈ 12–13 optimal viewpoints at ~99%; Crankshaft best ≈ 23–24 optimal viewpoints at ~99%. Octree-filtered vertex sampling and feature-functional patch sampling trade off: octree gives smaller candidate *sets* but a higher share of inadmissible candidates within them; patch sampling gives more intuitive threshold control and better distribution (Sect. 9.1.3, 9.4).
- **Fig. 10** (coverage-vs-#viewpoints convergence curves, one panel per method-family × object) shows patch sampling runs are tightly clustered ("no outliers causing the envelope to spread"), i.e. **consistent** across parameter choices, unlike space sampling.

**Timing** (Sect. 8, p.11 and Sect. 8.1): candidate-set *generation* itself is "in the magnitude of seconds" for every method tested — negligible. The bottleneck is the **visibility matrix** (ray tracing): **3.81 s average per viewpoint candidate**. For candidate sets capped at 1000 (sphere) or 1500 (other methods), that's roughly **1–1.6 hours of ray tracing per object**, run offline before the NBV optimizer, which itself then finishes in "under 10 seconds" for 1000 candidates. There is **no mention of an online/incremental variant** — this entire pipeline is framed as a one-time, offline, per-object precompute before deployment.

**Generate-and-use** converges to ≥90% coverage for all three objects within roughly 40–100 viewpoints depending on object (Fig. 11), repeated 100 times per object to get the convergence envelope.

**Automated vs. manual (Sect. 8.3/9.4, Fig. 12)**: two experts each planned full-coverage view sets by hand. Manual sets were **larger** in viewpoint count than the best automated result (e.g. Gear: expert 44 vs. best-automated 23 candidates, Fig. 12a–c) but exhibited a **spatial pattern** automated methods never produce — clusters placed for physical mountability (e.g. two fixed camera positions + turntable rotation for a symmetric gear), because experts implicitly optimize for ease of hardware implementation and traversal, not just coverage. Automated methods "exhibit no particular pattern in spatial distribution" (Sect. 9.4) — i.e., **no ordering or adjacency structure falls out of any of these algorithms**; if you want addressable/navigable structure, none of the methods here give it to you for free.

## Answers to / bearing on the branch questions

**Q1 (formal address per scheme)** — None of the schemes in this paper produce a persistent symbolic address at all; every candidate is stored as a raw 9D pose. The closest things to "address = object-part + local offset" (scheme b) are: **vertex sampling** (address = vertex id, pose = vertex position + fixed offset along normal) and **patch sampling** (address = subdivision-tree leaf segment, pose = segment-center pivot + offset along analytic normal). **Space sampling** is scheme (a)-like only in its *generation* step (index into a tessellated sphere) but that index is discarded once repositioning happens (Sect. 4.1.2) — post-repositioning, sphere-cell identity carries no further meaning. **No scheme here is (c)** — there is no relational/graph node-ID address anywhere; this paper simply doesn't do that kind of structure (see absence note below).

**Q2 (minimal scheme where "orthogonal view of face 3" is a native address)** — **Patch sampling** is the candidate: address = the subdivided B-spline segment (itself addressable as a path down the recursive quadtree, or just "segment i of surface j"), pose given in closed form by pivot + `d·normal` (companion-paper eq. 6). It does **not** strictly require CAD features — it works from a triangulated mesh via Eck–Hoppe quad remeshing + B-spline fit — but per the companion paper's stated caveat, fit quality (and thus placement quality) degrades under scan noise/incorrect topology, which is exactly our survey-cloud situation (mm-level pose error, depth dropout, ~4 cm fused-vs-single-view offset per our framing doc). **This paper does not test that failure mode**, so "does it tolerate primitives fitted to a noisy cloud" is answered by absence: no evidence given either way, and the one paper in this family that comments on noise tolerance (the companion) flags it as a real risk, not a solved problem.

**Q3 (does address need to encode pose)** — Not applicable/no evidence either way: this paper has no discrete-address layer to test against a PAMDP-style split. Its "address" (list index post-NBV) carries zero semantic content; the pose is the primary and only representation. If anything, this is a data point for the *opposite* extreme from the PAMDP hypothesis — a system that skips symbolic addressing entirely and is fine, provided you don't need a downstream agent to reason about *which* view it's requesting before execution (which our agent does need).

**Q4 (stable/meaningful ordering)** — Explicitly answered negatively for automated methods: NBV list order is greedy-coverage order (an artifact of the set-cover heuristic), not spatial. Sect. 9.4: "automatically created viewpoints exhibit no particular pattern in spatial distribution." Only manual/expert planning has an implicit spatially-meaningful order (turntable replication groups), and that's a human authoring convenience, not a property of the object-space-exploration algorithms.

**Q5 (adjacency definitions found)** — One concrete, computable definition: **octree-leaf co-membership** (Sect. 4.2.2) — two candidates are "neighbors" if they land in the same octree leaf (spatial cell adjacency on the vertex-sampled point set), used purely to prune near-duplicates by an orientation-angle threshold φ, not to support navigation. That's a literal instance of "cell neighbour in a tiling" from the branch's adjacency enumeration, computable on any point set (would transfer to our PLY). No visual-event-boundary or k-NN-on-sphere definitions appear in this paper.

**Q11 (pruning mechanisms)** — Two of the three named mechanisms appear directly: **similarity merging** (octree filtering, Sect. 4.2.2 — literal duplicate-orientation pruning) and **hierarchical refinement** (patch-sampling's recursive quadtree subdivision by feature functional, Sect. 4.3/Alg. 1–2 in companion paper). **Entity-based restriction to a region of interest** does *not* appear in this survey (it's in the companion Mosbach paper's Sect. 4.6, ROI functional, which this survey does not reuse or re-test) — worth noting since branch Q11 explicitly wants an ROI-restriction precedent; it exists one paper over, not here.

**Q12 (variable arity / stable meaning vs. stable count)** — Table 1/2 numbers directly falsify "stable count": the *same method* (e.g., exact-curvature patch sampling) yields ~33 optimal viewpoints on Gear, ~12 on Hirth, ~23 on Crankshaft — count tracks object complexity, as the paper explicitly wants (R2/R4, "adaptive"/"scalable coverage redundancy"). No method in this paper claims or delivers a bounded/uniform cardinality across shapes; cardinality is a *dependent variable* of geometry by design.

**Q13/Q16 (bounded-cardinality guarantee)** — Not offered, and the paper's design philosophy (R2, R4 in Sect. 3) is explicitly the opposite: redundancy/coverage should scale *with* object complexity, not be capped. If our RQ3 wants "always between N and M addressed views," this paper gives no mechanism for that and its authors would likely consider that goal in tension with R2/R4.

**Q15 (infeasibility handling / precedent for "nearest feasible" fallback)** — Concrete precedent, directly reusable for Branch 4: **constraint-aware sphere sampling** (Sect. 4.1.2) repairs an infeasible fixed-distance/fixed-direction candidate by ray-casting along its direction vector to the object surface (or convex hull) and moving the focal point there — and *optionally* also re-orients `d = −n` to align with the local surface normal, which is functionally identical to "snap to the orthogonal view." If the ray never hits anything, "the viewpoint remains unchanged" (i.e., stays in the candidate list, uncorrected, most likely to fail visibility ray-tracing later). Separately, the NBV optimizer's own admissibility filter (Sect. 5.1) **drops** candidates that fail focusing-distance/incidence-angle checks in the image center *before* they ever reach ray tracing — i.e., truly infeasible candidates **vanish from the menu**; they are not kept with a flagged/degraded pose. So: two different fallback behaviors coexist in one paper — *reposition-and-reorient toward feasible* for the repairable case, *silently vanish* for the unrepairable case. Neither produces a "flagged, still-addressable-but-degraded" option.

**Q17 (build cost / when)** — Answered with real numbers (above): candidate generation ~seconds; visibility-matrix ray tracing ~3.81 s/candidate × up to 1000–1500 candidates ≈ **~1–1.6 hours, offline, per object**; NBV selection <10 s. Everything is framed as **precomputed offline before deployment** — there is no incremental/online variant discussed anywhere in the paper. This is a hard mismatch with our "survey-then-inspect in one session" loop unless the visibility/coverage step can be replaced by something far cheaper for primitive-level geometry (boxes/cylinders), which is plausible for us but is not something this paper demonstrates.

**Q18 (benchmark: geometry-conditioned vs. uniform sphere, same task, with a metric)** — **Yes, this paper is exactly that benchmark**, and arguably the closest thing to the auxiliary-goal benchmark our project wants to build. It compares uniform/fixed-distance sphere sampling directly against vertex- and patch-based (geometry-conditioned) sampling, using a single shared downstream selection algorithm (greedy NBV over a ray-traced visibility matrix) and a single metric (% surface coverage, plus viewpoint count and inadmissible-candidate rate), across three non-trivial CAD objects (Table 1–4, Fig. 10). Result: uniform sphere sampling loses on every axis (coverage achievable, inadmissible-candidate rate, run-to-run consistency). **Caveat for reuse**: the metric is pure *coverage*, not *orthogonality-to-surface* (our project's actual quality criterion per the framing doc); objects are clean CAD meshes, not noisy fused point clouds; and "geometry-conditioned" here means "denser sampling near curvature," not "one view per named face/primitive." So it's strong existence-proof precedent for "benchmark uniform-sphere against geometry-aware," but not a template we can lift wholesale for an orthogonality-scored, primitive-addressed benchmark.

**Q17/liftability** — What's liftable and at what cost, per family: **space sampling** = trivial, any sphere sampler works (healpy/h3-style), but the paper's own conclusion is *don't* (Sect. 9.1.1). **Vertex sampling** = needs a mesh with normals; direct vertex sampling and octree filtering are both simple to reimplement (open3d-level operations: vertex normals + a spatial partition), and would run on a mesh reconstructed from our fused PLY (e.g. via Poisson reconstruction) rather than needing CAD. **Patch sampling** = the heaviest lift: needs quad remeshing + B-spline fit + fairing optimization (Eck–Hoppe, least-squares + thin-plate energy) — a real implementation project, not a drop-in library call, and per the companion paper's own caveat it degrades on noisy/low-quality input, which is a real risk for our survey cloud. No off-the-shelf library names (healpy, h3, MVTorch, Open3D) appear anywhere in this paper; the authors built and released their own tool (GitLab link above), CC BY 4.0.

## Explicit absences — what this paper does *not* address (first-class negative findings)

- **No aspect graphs, view graphs, or any qualitative/relational viewpoint-space structure anywhere in the paper or its ~19 references.** The related-work section (Sect. 2, citing Cowan & Kovesi, Tarabanis et al., Scott et al.) frames the field's history purely in terms of *generate-and-test vs. synthesis* approaches to **coverage optimization**, never in terms of aspect graphs, visual-event partitions, or symbolic viewpoint-space topology. This is a strong, citable data point that the *inspection-planning* literature and the *aspect-graph* literature are separate traditions that this survey (despite being the best modern survey of candidate-view-space construction) does not bridge — relevant to branch Qs 8–10 on aspect-graph death causes and whether this literature inherits them (it simply never engages with that literature at all).
- **No addressing/naming scheme for individual viewpoints survives past generation.** Even the two families with an implicit "address" (vertex id; subdivision-segment id) discard that identity once a pose is computed and placed in an unordered candidate pool for NBV selection — nothing downstream (Sect. 5's Algorithm 1) reasons about or preserves candidate identity/relationships, only visibility-matrix coverage.
- **No canonical-frame or cross-object-consistent-naming machinery.** Object coordinate origin is used ("relative to the object's origin," Sect. 4) but never discussed as needing disambiguation (no front/side/up convention debate) — the paper's objects (gear, hirth, crankshaft) are all planned independently with no claim of transferable per-face semantics across object classes.
- **No seam/degenerate-region naming.** B-spline patch stitching (C⁰-continuity constraints) is mentioned only as an implementation necessity (via the companion paper), never as a source of "which face am I on" ambiguity requiring its own address.

These absences matter for the branch's synthesis: this is the strongest available survey of *how to generate and select* view **candidates**, but it offers **no precedent at all** for *addressing/naming* them for an external agent to reason about before moving — which is precisely the gap RQ3's cross-shape addressing question (branch Q20) is probing for. This paper's silence on addressing, taken together with its explicit engagement with (and rejection of) the uniform-sphere baseline on coverage grounds, supports treating cross-shape view-*addressing* (as opposed to view *generation/selection*, which is well-trodden) as a genuine gap.

## What transfers to our setting

- **Uniform-sphere-is-worst finding transfers directly**: our current `{h,v}` action space is structurally the paper's "fixed-distance sphere sampling," and this is the one family the paper explicitly benchmarks and rejects (Sect. 9.1.1, Table 3) — external confirmation of the framing doc's problem statement, with numbers (worst inadmissible-candidate rates of any family) rather than just intuition.
- **Reposition-to-surface-normal fallback** (Sect. 4.1.2) is a directly reusable pattern for Branch 4's infeasibility handling: ray-cast toward the object/convex-hull, snap the pose there, reorient to `−n` if you want the orthogonal view specifically.
- **Feature-functional-driven adaptive subdivision** (Sect. 4.3) is conceptually the right shape for a primitive-level rule (box face → uniform low functional → no subdivision needed; cylinder body → curvature-driven subdivision around the orbit) even though the concrete B-spline machinery is heavier than we likely need for primitives.
- **Coverage-as-metric infrastructure** (visibility matrix via ray tracing + greedy set cover, Sect. 5) is a viable off-the-shelf template for the auxiliary view-quality benchmark, modulo swapping the objective from coverage to orthogonality.

## What does not transfer, and why

- **The whole pipeline assumes a clean, complete 3D surface model** (CAD mesh or B-spline network), fit *offline* before any online use, and none of the three test objects have anything like our survey-cloud defects (pose error, dropout, 4 cm fusion offset). Patch sampling in particular is flagged (by the companion paper it depends on) as noise-sensitive.
- **No addressing/symbolic layer exists to adapt** — there's nothing here to lift for "does the address need to encode pose" or "is there a stable ordering," because the paper never builds that layer at all.
- **Offline compute budget (~1–1.6 h/object for visibility-matrix ray tracing) doesn't fit a single-session survey-then-inspect loop** without either caching per-object-class results or replacing ray-traced coverage with a much cheaper primitive-level orthogonality check.
- **Objective mismatch**: the paper optimizes *coverage*, not *"most orthogonal view of the surface"* — our actual quality bar (per Albert's framing) is a stricter, different criterion than anything this benchmark scores.
