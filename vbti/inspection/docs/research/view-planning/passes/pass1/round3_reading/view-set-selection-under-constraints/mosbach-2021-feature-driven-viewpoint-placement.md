## Core idea

The paper attacks **candidate generation**, not selection, and treats them as genuinely separate pipeline stages (Fig. 1: Virtual Model → Viewpoint Candidates → Selected Viewpoints → Path Planning → Inspection). Its contribution is stage 2 only. The method:

1. **Convert** an input triangle mesh into a network of $C^0$-continuous cubic B-spline surfaces (Sect. 3.1) via quad-mesh partitioning (Eck & Hoppe '96) + per-quad parameterization + least-squares fit with a thin-plate smoothing term (Eq. 2–4).
2. **Recursively subdivide** each B-spline's parameter domain $\Omega_0$ into quadrants until a chosen **feature functional** $E(S,\Omega)$, evaluated on the segment, drops below a threshold $t$ (Algorithm 1, Sect. 3.2). $E$ must be *additive* over disjoint partitions to guarantee monotone convergence to zero as $\Omega$ shrinks (the paper's formal correctness condition, stated right before Sect. 3).
3. **Place one viewpoint candidate per leaf segment**: pivot = surface evaluated at the segment's parameter-domain center $(u_c,v_c)$; camera position = pivot **moved along the surface normal** by a user-fixed distance $d$ (Eq. 6, Fig. 5). *By construction every candidate is exactly orthogonal to its own patch* — orthogonality is not an optimization target, it is baked into how a candidate is generated at all.
4. **Select a subset** with a standard greedy next-best-view (NBV) algorithm solving the classic Set Covering Problem (Eq. 11) against the *original* triangle mesh's visibility matrix (Sect. 5) — used only to validate/benchmark the candidate sets, explicitly not the paper's contribution.

The paper defines six interchangeable feature functionals (Sect. 4): curvature $E_\kappa$ (Eq. 7–9, exact $\kappa_1^2+\kappa_2^2$ integral), thin-plate energy $E_{TP}$ (Eq. 3, a cheap curvature proxy borrowed from variational surface design), max normal deviation $E_{ND}$, **max incidence angle $E_{IA}$** (Eq. "IA", the orthogonality-tolerance functional — directly answers branch Q7's framing), surface area $E_A$ (Eq. 10, an FOV/footprint-driven cap), and a manually painted region-of-interest indicator $E_{ROI}$. Multiple functionals can be combined by OR-ing their threshold tests (Sect. 3.2.2, demonstrated Fig. 9).

## Concrete results and numbers

- **Test objects (Sect. 6, Table 1):** cylinder (21,800 mesh triangles, 6 B-spline surfaces, 16×16 control points, Fig. 6), spring (51,800 triangles, 14 surfaces, 20×20 control points, Fig. 7), "tangle cube" (82,672 triangles, 452 surfaces, 6×6 control points, reconstructed from a 3D volumetric scan, Fig. 8).
- **Candidate counts vs. selected counts (Table 1)** — this is the paper's headline number, and it is large:
  - Cylinder: $n_{vpc}$ candidates range 36–48 across functionals; **selected** $n_{opt}$ = 10–24 (e.g., incidence-angle $t{=}60°$: 48 candidates → 22 selected; curvature $t{=}0.4t_{avg}$: 36 → 10).
  - Spring: $n_{vpc}$ = 194–482; $n_{opt}$ = 9–13 (e.g., thin-plate: 194 → 13; normal-deviation: 482 → 9).
  - Tangle cube: $n_{vpc}$ = 1325–1571; $n_{opt}$ = 32–38.
  - So even feature-driven candidate generation over-produces by roughly **3–13×** relative to what greedy set-cover selection actually keeps — the generation stage is deliberately generous; minimality is entirely the selection stage's job.
- **Timing breakdown (Table 1, three columns: SD = subdivision, RT = ray-tracing the visibility matrix, NBV = greedy selection):**
  - Cylinder: SD 0.001–12.5 s; RT ≈ 41–62 s; NBV 0.4–1.0 s.
  - Tangle cube: SD 0.008–25 s; **RT ≈ 2556–3103 s (43–52 minutes)**; NBV ≈ 235–290 s (4–5 min).
  - The dominant cost by orders of magnitude is **building the visibility matrix by ray-tracing candidates against the original high-poly mesh** (tens of thousands to ~82,700 triangles), not the feature-driven subdivision itself (ms–tens of s) and not the greedy selection algorithm applied to the resulting small candidate/coverage matrix.
- **Thin-plate vs. curvature discrepancy, quantified (Sect. 7.1, Fig. 11, Table 3):** on the cylinder, thin-plate energy puts **18.83%** of its total value on the (perfectly flat) top/bottom caps and only 49.59%+28.22%=77.81% on the genuinely curved short/long mantle sides, whereas exact curvature puts **0%** on the caps. Cause: B-spline parameterization of an elliptical cap introduces nonzero second derivatives (planar distortion) even though $\kappa_1=\kappa_2=0$ there. This is a load-bearing, quantified negative finding about the cheap proxy, not a hand-wave.
- **Stability / threshold sensitivity (Sect. 7.3, Fig. 13, Fig. 14):** for **non-additive** functionals (normal deviation, incidence angle), small threshold changes can flip between "no subdivision at all" and "several rounds of subdivision" on the same surface (explicit example: Fig. 7c/d, spring model's middle section). Fig. 14 shows angle-based functionals (red/blue curves) have non-monotonic "wiggles" over subdivision iterations, unlike the near-exponential monotone decay of additive integral functionals (thin-plate, curvature, area).
- **Qualitative comparison table (Table 2):** rates each functional on intuition-of-values, computational cost, meeting-design-objectives, and additivity. Region-of-interest and area are the only "+++"/cheap options; curvature is accurate but "− −" on cost; thin-plate is "+++"cheap but only "+" on meeting objectives; **normal deviation and incidence angle are marked "No" on additivity** — directly tied to the instability finding above.

## Formal problem statement for selection (Sect. 5, Eq. 11)

Given faces $F$ ($n_F$ triangles of the *original* mesh) and candidates $V=\{v_1,...,v_{n_{vpc}}\}$, build binary visibility matrix $A\in\{0,1\}^{n_F\times n_{vpc}}$ via ray tracing. The stated problem is a **weighted minimum Set Covering Problem**:
$$\min\sum_i c_i x_i,\quad c_i = \frac{1}{\sum_{k=1}^{n_F} a_{ki}},\quad \text{s.t. } Ax\ge b^{(F)}$$
with $b^{(F)}=(1,...,1)^T$ for full coverage — i.e., cost of a candidate is *inversely* proportional to how many faces it covers (cheap = high-coverage), biasing the LP toward big, efficient viewpoints. They cite Slavík's tight greedy-approximation analysis [25] for set cover, but then **do not solve Eq. 11 directly** — they run a plain rarest-face-first greedy NBV heuristic instead (start from the viewpoint that best covers the least-covered triangle, then repeatedly add the candidate covering the most remaining-uncovered triangles), explicitly because "the next best view approach is frequently used to solve this problem [11,14,22]" and comparability with prior work was the goal, not provable optimality. **No quality/orthogonality weighting is folded into the coverage objective at all** — $a_{ij}$ is a pure binary visibility indicator. Orthogonality/quality is entirely a property of *how a candidate was generated* (Eq. 6, camera on the normal line), never a term in the selection objective.

## Load-bearing assumptions

- **Requires a parametric (B-spline) model, and it must be clean.** The paper explicitly assumes "only a triangulated surface mesh of the object of interest is given" and *reconstructs* B-splines from it (Sect. 3.1, Fig. 3), but flags: "If the input data are obtained via 3D scanning... it will be essential to ensure highly accurate scanning with a low degree of noise to obtain high-quality B-spline models" (Sect. 3.1, paragraph before 3.2). The quad-partitioning step (Eck & Hoppe) further assumes disk-like topology and a mesh regular enough to Voronoi/Delaunay-tessellate.
- **No coverage guarantee from generation alone (Sect. 7.4, "Limitations"):** "the subdivision approach does not explicitly address the total coverage of an object" — the authors only claim it's *usually* fine in practice because subdivision is fine enough to leave at least one candidate everywhere; there is no proof.
- **Self-occlusion is only handled at the *selection* stage** (ray tracing naturally stops at first hit when building $A$), never at candidate-*generation* time.
- **No reachability, collision, or kinematic treatment anywhere** — stated explicitly as unaddressed future work (see below).
- **Candidate distance $d$ from the surface is a single manually chosen scalar per model** (Sect. 3.3) — not derived per-region from FOV/DOF, though the area functional (Eq. 10) is explicitly motivated as a proxy for "narrow camera frustum... limiting factors on the amount of surface processed from a single viewpoint" (Sect. 4.5) without ever giving the closed-form footprint-area-from-FOV-and-$d$ formula.

## What transfers to our setting

- **Decoupling "quality guaranteed by construction" from "coverage solved by set cover" is a clean, citable architectural precedent for branch Q1.** Mosbach's answer to "is it min-set-cover or quality-subject-to-a-floor" is effectively **both, but split across stages**: generation enforces per-candidate orthogonality/quality (Eq. 6 always places the camera on the normal), and only the *residual* combinatorial problem (which of these already-orthogonal candidates to keep) is posed as minimum set cover (Eq. 11, `Ax ≥ b`, not max-k-coverage — there is no view budget in the selection formulation itself). This is strong precedent for *not* trying to fuse orthogonality into a single weighted-coverage objective (relevant to Q2): Mosbach never attempts a quality-weighted coverage objective, so it offers no evidence either way on submodularity there, but it demonstrates a working alternative that sidesteps the question entirely.
- **Direct precedent numbers for "ring of views per cylinder, incidence-angle-bounded" (branch Q5, Q6, Q7).** Fig. 6d: cylinder mantle subdivided under $E_{IA}$ at $t=60°, d=20 \Rightarrow n_{vpc}=48$ candidates arranged as rings around the curved mantle plus a few on the flat caps — a concrete, citable "the number of ring-views is set directly by an incidence-angle tolerance" data point. Table 1(a) full ladder for the same object across all six functionals gives comparably-sized candidate/selected counts (36–48 → 10–24), useful as an order-of-magnitude sanity check for our own primitive-level counts.
- **The area functional (Eq. 10, Sect. 4.5) is the closest published tie between FOV/frustum and view count/segment size**, even though the closed-form isn't spelled out: "inspection pipeline characteristics such as a narrow camera frustum might impose limiting factors on the amount of surface that can be processed from a single viewpoint... a subdivision by area will always ensure a certain sampling resolution." This licenses (but doesn't fully derive) "view count per primitive ~ surface area / FOV-limited footprint at working distance $d$" as a defensible closed-form starting point for branch Q6.
- **At our instance size, exact solving is trivially justified (branch Q3).** Mosbach's own numbers are the strongest evidence available: their *smallest* case (cylinder, 36–48 candidates, 10–24 selected, from 21,800 mesh triangles) already needs only NBV's 0.4–1.0 s, and even the worst case (tangle cube, 1325–1571 candidates) only costs 4–5 minutes of NBV time — and **that** cost is dominated by ray-tracing an 82,672-triangle mesh (43–52 min), not the combinatorial search itself. Our stated instance size (tens of candidates, tens of regions) is smaller than even their cylinder case, and we have no comparably expensive raw-mesh ray-tracing step to build a coverage matrix from scratch at that scale. This directly supports the branch's ILP/FPT hypothesis: if greedy NBV is near-instant at their scale, exact ILP is certainly tractable at ours.
- **The additivity property (formalized right before Sect. 3, used throughout) is a reusable design rule**: any per-region scoring functional we define for our own pipeline should satisfy additivity (partitioning a region monotonically decreases the summed score) if we want stable, threshold-percentage-of-average behavior and to avoid the instability documented in Sect. 7.3 — this is a concrete, transferable engineering constraint, not just this paper's internal concern.
- **Combining functionals via threshold-OR (Sect. 3.2.2, Fig. 9)** is a simple, reusable mechanism if we want e.g. "subdivide further if curvature is high OR incidence angle would exceed our tolerance OR area exceeds the FOV footprint" as a single primitive-level rule.

## What does NOT transfer, and why (negative findings — first class)

1. **Input assumption breaks on our survey cloud.** Our geometry is a fused, defect-bearing point cloud with ~mm pose error, per-view depth dropout, and a measured ~4 cm fused-vs-single-view offset (per framing doc). Mosbach's pipeline requires reconstructing a *smooth*, topologically clean B-spline network first (Sect. 3.1) and explicitly warns that scanning noise degrades B-spline quality — there is no discussion of how the method behaves under real depth-sensor artifacts, holes, or actual surface defects (which we specifically want to *see*, not smooth away). Fitting a thin-plate-regularized B-spline over a defect (a dent, scratch, or hole) risks smoothing out exactly the feature we need a view of. This is a structural mismatch, not a minor gap.
2. **No reachability/kinematic/collision treatment at all (branch Q8–Q12).** Sect. 7.4, verbatim: "the algorithm does not deal with self-occlusions and collisions of viewpoint candidates with the object's geometry. Holes or tight cavities still pose a challenge." There is no fallback ladder, no capability map, no IK filtering, no joint-space optimization — reachability is not merely simplified here, it is completely absent from candidate generation. (Self-occlusion *is* partially handled, but only downstream in the coverage-matrix ray tracing at selection time, not at generation time — a candidate placed at $d$ along a normal inside a concave cavity is never itself checked for validity.) This paper contributes nothing toward the branch's headline fallback-ladder question beyond confirming the gap exists and is explicitly acknowledged as unaddressed future work by the original authors.
3. **No learned component, no primitive-decomposition framing, no benchmark-style evaluation against ground-truth defects.** The method is purely geometric/deterministic; it says nothing about learned NBV comparisons (Q14) or self-acquired defective clouds as the input modality (Q15). It also doesn't decompose objects into primitives in the box/cylinder/etc. sense we mean — it fits *one* continuous B-spline network to the whole object regardless of primitive type, so it is evidence *for* the general "feature-driven density, not per-vertex" principle (Q5) but not a worked example of primitive-decomposition-conditioned planning (Q18 gap likely still stands after this paper).
4. **The formal selection algorithm (Eq. 11) and the actually-run algorithm (greedy NBV) don't match.** The paper poses a weighted set-cover LP but solves an unweighted greedy heuristic instead, purely for comparability with prior work — worth knowing so we don't cite Eq. 11 as "the algorithm used" when checking their numbers; the achieved $n_{opt}$ counts in Table 1 come from the greedy heuristic, not from solving Eq. 11 to optimality.
5. **Coverage target is full coverage of the ORIGINAL mesh, no budget/k parameter in selection.** There is no max-k-coverage variant tested; Algorithm 2 (iterative subdivision, Sect. 3.2.3) gives a fixed-budget mode only for *candidate generation* (stop once $n_t$ candidates are reached, always splitting the highest-scoring remaining segment), not for the final selected set. If we need a hard cap on the *final* VLM-facing action-space size (RQ3/RQ4's small-set pressure), Mosbach's Algorithm 2 is a transferable idea for capping candidates but offers no analog for capping the post-selection set — that would still need our own mechanism (e.g., true max-k-coverage instead of min-set-cover in Eq. 11's role).
